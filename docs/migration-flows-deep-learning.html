How Gaskin and Abel (2026) estimate bilateral flows nobody measured, by embedding an accounting identity in a recurrent network, and a small replication on a synthetic world

Nobody counts how many people move from one country to another. Some countries register arrivals and departures, most do not, and the ones that do use different definitions of a “move”. Border crossings count tourists. Population registers count only people who bother to deregister. Censuses count how many foreign-born people live in a country on one day every five or ten years, which says almost nothing about how many arrived and left in between. Yet researchers, governments, and humanitarian agencies need annual, bilateral, country-of-birth-resolved flows, because that is the quantity that responds to wars, recessions, and visa rules.

In 2026, Thomas Gaskin and Guy Abel published an answer in Nature (Gaskin and Abel, 2026): a dataset of origin-destination migration flows and stocks between 230 countries and regions, disaggregated by country of birth, from 1990 onward, with uncertainty bounds, produced by a deep recurrent neural network. This chapter is about how the problem was set up, because the setup is the transferable lesson. The authors did not find a labelled dataset of true flows and fit a regression to it, because no such dataset exists. They wrote down the one fact that is certain (people are conserved: a stock changes only through births, deaths, and moves), built that accounting identity into the training loop of a neural network, and asked the network to find the unobserved flow tensor that makes every partial, noisy, mutually inconsistent observation come out approximately right. The method belongs to the family called scientific machine learning or differentiable physics (Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis, 2019; Rackauckas et al., 2020), applied to a social system instead of a physical one.

The thesis of this chapter in one paragraph

When the quantity you need is never observed directly, but several aggregates of it are observed by different noisy instruments, do not discard the instruments that disagree. Write the exact relation between the hidden quantity and each aggregate, put those relations inside a differentiable model, and train the model on the aggregates. Add three ingredients that make this work in practice: a recurrent latent state so that today’s flows can depend on the whole past (Section 20.3), a variable transform and an uncertainty weighting so that no single huge value or unreliable source dominates the loss (Section 20.4 and Section 20.5), and an ensemble so that the answer comes with an honest spread (Section 20.13). The chapter derives each piece, implements it in a tested tri-language library, and replicates the idea on a synthetic world of eight countries where the truth is known, so that we can measure what the method recovers and where it fails.

The structure is as follows. We first state what the paper observed and what it estimated (Section 20.1), derive the accounting identity and count unknowns against observations (Section 20.2), describe the recurrent network, why memory matters, how it relates to the RNN, LSTM, and GRU, and how gradients travel through its two recurrent paths (Section 20.3, Section 20.3.2, Section 20.3.3), then the scaling, loss, and demographic balancing (Section 20.4, Section 20.5, Section 20.6). A synthetic world follows (Section 20.7), with training and an honest recovery check (Section 20.8 and Section 20.9), and a measurement of the trained model’s gradient paths with a gated ablation (Section 20.10). We then cover elasticities, initial-stock calibration, and uncertainty (Section 20.11, Section 20.12, Section 20.13), what to change to rerun on the real data (Section 20.14), and the reference implementation in Python, Julia, and Rust (Section 20.16).

What the paper did, and what this chapter does

Everything labelled “paper” below is from Gaskin and Abel (2026), as described in the arXiv version of the manuscript (arXiv:2506.22821). Everything labelled “toy” is this chapter’s own small experiment on simulated data. The toy shares the paper’s structure but is orders of magnitude smaller (eight countries, a few thousand parameters, five ensemble members), runs on a laptop in a few minutes, and was never meant to reproduce the paper’s numbers. Table 20.1 lists the differences once, and the results section repeats the caveat where it matters.

20.1 The Problem: Four Instruments, None of Them the Quantity

Index countries by \(i\) (country of birth), \(j\) (country of origin of a move, meaning where the person lived before moving), and \(k\) (destination). The object of interest is the flow tensor \(T_{ijk}(t)\), the number of people born in \(i\) who moved from \(j\) to \(k\) in year \(t\). Setting \(i = j\) gives moves by natives of \(j\) leaving home; \(i = k\) gives people returning to their country of birth; \(i\) distinct from both gives onward migration, such as a person born in Syria moving from Turkey to Germany. The aggregates the world actually publishes are sums of this tensor:

  • Migrant stocks \(S_{ij}(t)\): the number of people born in \(i\) living in \(j\) at time \(t\). The UN Department of Economic and Social Affairs publishes these every five years, with revisions that do not agree across editions.
  • Total flows \(F_{jk}(t) = \sum_i T_{ijk}(t)\) for a few corridors: harmonised intra-European estimates (the paper uses the Quantmig database, which comes with uncertainty), national statistics from a few countries, and Facebook-derived estimates for 2019 to 2022.
  • Net migration \(\mu_j(t)\) for about 30 countries from national statistical offices. The UN’s own net migration series is computed as a demographic residual (population change minus births plus deaths), so the paper does not train on it, and instead uses it as a comparison.

Each instrument measures a different slice with a different definition, and the paper’s first figure shows that even for a single corridor the sending country’s deregistrations and the receiving country’s registrations differ by large factors. The estimation problem is therefore not “denoise one dataset”. It is reconcile several datasets that were never meant to agree, through a latent object that explains all of them.

20.2 The Accounting Identity and the Counting Problem

The one thing we know for certain is that people are conserved. Let \(B_j(t)\) be births in country \(j\) and \(\mu_j^{\text{d}}(t)\) the mortality rate there (written \(m_j\) below to keep \(\mu\) free for net migration). The stock of people born in \(i\) living in \(j\) evolves as

\[ \frac{\partial S_{ij}}{\partial t} = \delta_{ij} B_j - m_j S_{ij} + \sum_{k} \left( T_{ikj} - T_{ijk} \right), \tag{20.1}\]

where \(\delta_{ij}\) is the Kronecker delta. The three terms say, in order: every birth in \(j\) adds to the native-born stock \(S_{jj}\); deaths remove a fraction of every stock; and the third term sums arrivals of \(i\)-born people into \(j\) from every other country \(k\) minus departures from \(j\) to every \(k\). Aggregating the tensor gives the quantities that are actually observed,

\[ F_{jk} = \sum_i T_{ijk}, \qquad \mu_j = \sum_k F_{kj} - \sum_k F_{jk}. \tag{20.2}\]

In discrete annual form, which is what the code integrates, \(S_{ij}(t+1) = S_{ij}(t) + \delta_{ij} B_j - m_j S_{ij}(t) + \sum_k (T_{ikj} - T_{ijk})\). The function stock_step implements exactly this, od_flows computes \(F\), and net_migration computes \(\mu\).

Conservation. Summing Equation 20.1 over all \(i\) and \(j\), the migration term telescopes: each flow \(T_{ijk}\) appears once with a plus sign (arrival in \(k\)) and once with a minus sign (departure from \(j\)), so

\[ \sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial S_{ij}}{\partial t} = \sum_j B_j - \sum_j m_j P_j, \qquad P_j = \sum_i S_{ij}, \tag{20.3}\]

and likewise \(\sum_j \mu_j = 0\). Migration moves people between cells but never creates or destroys them. This is not a modelling assumption that data could contradict. It is an identity, and it is what makes training possible: any flow tensor the network proposes automatically implies a full set of stocks, a full set of net migration figures, and a full set of corridor flows that are consistent with one another, even though the observed versions of those quantities are not.

The next cell checks the identity on the synthetic world built later in the chapter, and counts unknowns against observations. The counting is the heart of why the problem is hard.

The toy has 484 observations for 8,960 unknowns, about 0.05 per unknown, and the paper’s problem is far more underdetermined, because the number of unknowns grows as the cube of the number of countries while observations grow roughly with the square. The system of equations has many solutions. What selects among them is the other ingredient: the network ties the flows of different edges and different years together through shared parameters and shared covariates, so that the answer must be a smooth function of covariates rather than an arbitrary table. The accounting identity supplies the constraints; the network supplies the inductive bias. Gaskin and Abel are explicit that the problem is ill-posed in this sense and use an ensemble precisely to measure how much the answer depends on which of the many fitting solutions the optimiser lands on.

Classical methods choose different inductive biases. Stock differencing sets the flow from \(i\) to \(j\) to the positive part of \(S_{ij}(t + 5) - S_{ij}(t)\) divided by five (Abel, 2013). It assumes people move only between their birth country and a destination, ignores births and deaths, drops negative changes, and can only produce five-year averages. Demographic accounting methods estimate a three-dimensional flow table subject to the stock differences (Abel, 2013; Azose and Raftery, 2019; Abel and Cohen, 2019), but treat stocks as exact and use no covariates, so they cannot produce annual flows or explain them. Gravity models regress flows on covariates and can in principle give annual flows, but the paper argues they treat people as Markovian, reacting only to this year’s covariates, which is hard to defend for long-run economic and political conditions. The recurrent network is the paper’s attempt to keep the covariates of the gravity approach and the exactness of the accounting approach while adding memory.

20.3 The Recurrent Network: Flows With Memory

The paper’s mapping takes the covariates of the three countries involved, \(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{ijk}(t)\), together with a hidden state \(\mathbf{z}_{ijk}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{Z}\), and returns a log flow estimate and the next hidden state:

\[ u_\theta\!\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{ijk}(t), \mathbf{z}_{ijk}(t)\right) = \left( \log \hat T_{ijk}(t),\; \mathbf{z}_{ijk}(t+1) \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 + Z}. \tag{20.4}\]

The latent state starts at zero and “is a memory of past changes and their effects on the present flow estimate”. The paper cites the encoder-decoder papers of Cho et al. (2014) and Sutskever, Vinyals, and Le (2014) for its recurrent network, but the mapping in Equation 20.4 has no gates and no separate memory cell, so it is not a long short-term memory network (LSTM; Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) or a gated recurrent unit (GRU). It is a simple recurrent network in the sense of Elman (1990): part of the network’s activity at year \(t\) is copied back and read as an extra input at year \(t + 1\), and the same network is applied independently to every edge \((i, j, k)\), with the recurrence running over years. Section 20.3.2 places it precisely among the vanilla RNN, the LSTM, and the GRU. The covariates include the migrant stocks \(S_{ij}\) and \(S_{ik}\), which are not observed every year. In intermediate years the stocks fed to the network come from the network’s own earlier predictions, via the accounting identity. That is a second recursion: output at year \(t\) determines stocks at \(t + 1\), which are inputs at \(t + 1\).

Why bother with memory? The paper’s argument is that the decision to leave is “not merely predicated on the current economic climate: crises from past years can influence a person’s decision”. There are mechanical reasons too: diaspora networks, remittances, and visa regimes all carry the past forward. A feedforward network that maps this year’s covariates to this year’s flows has no way to represent a shock that raised flows two years ago and is still decaying. The toy builds this effect into the ground truth on purpose, so that we can test whether the latent state captures it (Section 20.9).

Two details matter for implementation. First, the output is \(\log \hat T\), so \(\hat T = \exp(\cdot) > 0\) and the model cannot propose negative flows; the paper finishes the network with a CELU activation (Barron, 2017), which is linear for positive inputs. With a positive scale \(\alpha\), as in the toy’s \(\alpha = 8\), it saturates smoothly at \(-\alpha\) below, giving a floor on \(\log T\). Second, training runs through the whole simulated history, so gradients flow backward through every year of the recursion (backpropagation through time; Werbos, 1990), and the full loss is computed on aggregates over all edges. In the toy the whole 20-year rollout for all 448 edges is one forward pass, so this is cheap. At the paper’s scale it is the reason training is a serious computational project. Section 20.3.3 works out which paths that gradient takes and when it vanishes.

The network is deliberately small. Nothing in the method depends on size, and a small network makes the replication fast and the failure modes visible.

def _make_net(torch, n_in: int, cfg: TrainConfig):

20.3.1 The recurrence the library implements

Write \(e = (i, j, k)\) for an edge, \(E = n^2 (n - 1)\) for the number of edges (448 in the toy), \(\boldsymbol{\phi}^e_t \in \mathbb{R}^{10}\) for the edge’s standardised covariates in year \(t\) (populations and GDP of origin and destination, distance, common language, the two conflict indicators, and the two native indicators \(\delta_{ij}\), \(\delta_{ik}\)), and \(Z\) for the latent width. The network sees the two stock cells of an edge through a logarithm and a standardisation,

\[ \sigma(S) = \frac{\log\big(1 + \max(S, 0)\big) - \mu_s}{s_s}, \tag{20.5}\]

where \(\mu_s\) and \(s_s\) are the mean and standard deviation of the log observed stocks. One year of the model for edge \(e\) is then

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x}^e_t &= \big[\boldsymbol{\phi}^e_t;\ \sigma(S_{ij,t});\ \sigma(S_{ik,t});\ \mathbf{z}^e_t\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{12 + Z}, \\ \mathbf{h}^{(0)} &= \mathbf{x}^e_t, \qquad \mathbf{h}^{(l)} = \tanh\big(W_l \mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}_l\big), \quad l = 1, \dots, L, \\ \mathbf{o}^e_t &= \operatorname{CELU}_\alpha\big(W_o \mathbf{h}^{(L)} + \mathbf{b}_o\big) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 + Z}, \\ u^e_t &= \log \hat T^e_t = o^e_{t,0}, \qquad \mathbf{z}^e_{t+1} = \big(o^e_{t,1}, \dots, o^e_{t,Z}\big), \qquad \mathbf{z}^e_0 = \mathbf{0}, \end{aligned} \tag{20.6}\]

with \(\operatorname{CELU}_\alpha(a) = \max(0, a) + \min\big(0, \alpha(e^{a/\alpha} - 1)\big)\) applied coordinate by coordinate. The toy uses \(L = 3\) layers of 32 units, \(Z = 8\), and \(\alpha = 8\) (TrainConfig); the paper uses 7 layers of 60 units and \(Z = 100\). The flows close the loop through the accounting identity, with births proportional to the population of the country of residence, \(B_j = b_j \sum_{i'} S_{i'j}\):

\[ S_{t+1} = A(S_t) + R(\hat T_t), \qquad \big[A(S)\big]_{ij} = (1 - m_j)\, S_{ij} + \delta_{ij}\, b_j \sum_{i'} S_{i'j}, \qquad \big[R(T)\big]_{ij} = \sum_k T_{ikj} - \sum_k T_{ijk}. \tag{20.7}\]

Two features of these equations decide everything that follows. First, the latent block is read off the output layer and copied back into the input layer one year later. Copied-back units are Elman’s context units, and the only difference from his network is what sits between the copy and its next value: Elman’s network has one hidden layer, so a context vector passes through one affine map and one squashing function per step, while here \(\mathbf{z}\) passes through \(L + 1 = 4\) affine maps, three \(\tanh\) layers, and a CELU. Pascanu et al. (2014) call this a deep transition recurrent network. The vanilla RNN of Chapter 222 is the special case \(L = 0\), with \(\tanh\) in place of the CELU. Second, the network’s state is not all overwritten each year. The latent coordinates are, but the stocks are carried forward by a fixed affine map \(A\), and only the increment \(R(\hat T_t)\) is produced by the network.

20.3.2 Where it sits among the RNN, the LSTM, and the GRU

Table 222.1 in Chapter 222 compares the three classical recurrent cells by the state they carry, how they update it, and the direct term of the Jacobian of that update, which controls how gradient travels backward through time. The same three questions applied to this chapter’s model give Table 20.2. The model carries two states at once, and they answer the questions differently.

Read as a whole, the network is an Elman recurrence in its latent block with something like an LSTM cell running beside it. The stock table plays the part of the LSTM’s cell state \(c_t\): it is carried forward additively, with a coefficient \(1 - m_j \approx 0.99\) where the LSTM has its forget gate \(f_t\), and the flows play the part of the gated write \(i_t \odot \tilde c_t\). The difference is who controls the carry. An LSTM learns \(f_t\) and can choose what to keep; here demography sets the carry, and the network can use it only indirectly, by writing flows whose effect on the stocks it later reads back through Equation 20.5. Note also that the “no memory” ablation of Section 20.8 zeroes \(\mathbf{z}\) every year but keeps the stock path, so it is memoryless only in its latent block.

20.3.3 Backpropagation through time along two paths

Training minimises the loss of Equation 20.18, which depends on the flows of every year, by differentiating through the whole 20-year rollout (backpropagation through time; Werbos, 1990). Stack the state of year \(t\) as \(X_t = (\mathbf{z}_t, S_t)\), where \(\mathbf{z}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{EZ}\) collects the latent states of all edges and \(S_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n^2}\) is the stock table. Equation 20.6 and Equation 20.7 define a map \(X_{t+1} = \Phi_t(X_t)\). For a loss term \(\ell\) that depends on the log flows \(\mathbf{u}_\tau\) of a late year \(\tau\), the chain rule gives its gradient with respect to the state of an earlier year \(s < \tau\) as

\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial X_s} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{u}_\tau}\, \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}_\tau}{\partial X_\tau}\, \mathcal{J}_{\tau-1}\, \mathcal{J}_{\tau-2} \cdots \mathcal{J}_s, \qquad \mathcal{J}_t = \frac{\partial X_{t+1}}{\partial X_t}. \tag{20.8}\]

The one-year Jacobian of the network. For one edge, differentiate Equation 20.6 layer by layer. Each layer contributes its weight matrix and the derivative of its activation, \(\tanh'(a) = 1 - \tanh^2(a)\), and for the output layer

\[ \operatorname{CELU}'_\alpha(a) = \begin{cases} 1 & a > 0, \\ e^{a/\alpha} & a \le 0, \end{cases} \]

so the Jacobian of the output with respect to the input is the product

\[ D^e_t = \frac{\partial \mathbf{o}^e_t}{\partial \mathbf{x}^e_t} = \mathrm{diag}\big(\operatorname{CELU}'_\alpha(\mathbf{a}_o)\big)\, W_o\, \mathrm{diag}\big(1 - (\mathbf{h}^{(L)})^2\big)\, W_L \cdots \mathrm{diag}\big(1 - (\mathbf{h}^{(1)})^2\big)\, W_1, \tag{20.9}\]

with \(\mathbf{a}_o = W_o \mathbf{h}^{(L)} + \mathbf{b}_o\). Its rows split into the flow (row 0) and the latent block (rows 1 to \(Z\)), and its columns into the covariates, the two stock inputs, and the latent inputs. That defines four per-edge blocks: \(P^e_t = \partial \mathbf{z}^e_{t+1} / \partial \mathbf{z}^e_t\) (\(Z \times Z\)), \(\mathbf{g}^e_t = \partial u^e_t / \partial \mathbf{z}^e_t\) (\(1 \times Z\)), and the derivatives of \(\mathbf{z}^e_{t+1}\) and \(u^e_t\) with respect to the two stock inputs, which pick up the factor \(\partial \sigma / \partial S = 1 / \big(s_s (1 + S)\big)\) from Equation 20.5.

The one-year Jacobian of the state. Collect the per-edge blocks over all edges: \(P_t = \mathrm{blockdiag}_e(P^e_t)\) of size \(EZ \times EZ\), \(G_t = \mathrm{blockdiag}_e(\mathbf{g}^e_t)\) of size \(E \times EZ\), \(Q_t = \partial \mathbf{z}_{t+1} / \partial S_t\) of size \(EZ \times n^2\), and \(H_t = \partial \mathbf{u}_t / \partial S_t\) of size \(E \times n^2\). The flow is \(\hat T = e^{u}\), so \(\partial \hat T / \partial u = \mathrm{diag}(\hat T)\), and \(R\) is linear, \(R(\hat T) = M \hat T\), where column \(e = (i, j, k)\) of \(M \in \mathbb{R}^{n^2 \times E}\) has \(+1\) in row \((i, k)\) (an arrival) and \(-1\) in row \((i, j)\) (a departure). Writing \(A\) also for the \(n^2 \times n^2\) matrix of the linear map in Equation 20.7, differentiating \(X_{t+1} = \Phi_t(X_t)\) block by block gives

\[ \mathcal{J}_t = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{z}_{t+1}}{\partial \mathbf{z}_t} & \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{z}_{t+1}}{\partial S_t} \\[3mm] \dfrac{\partial S_{t+1}}{\partial \mathbf{z}_t} & \dfrac{\partial S_{t+1}}{\partial S_t} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} P_t & Q_t \\ M\, \mathrm{diag}(\hat T_t)\, G_t & A + M\, \mathrm{diag}(\hat T_t)\, H_t \end{pmatrix}. \tag{20.10}\]

Multiplying out Equation 20.8, every term is a path through these blocks. A path that stays in the top-left block is the latent path, one that stays in the bottom-right block is the stock path, and a path that switches between them is mixed. Figure 20.2 draws one year.

When the latent path vanishes or explodes. \(P_t\) is block diagonal, so each edge’s latent memory is private: it never mixes with another edge’s latent state. Because \(0 < \tanh' \le 1\) and \(0 < \operatorname{CELU}'_\alpha \le 1\), the diagonal factors in Equation 20.9 have spectral norm at most one, and

\[ \big\| P^e_t \big\|_2 \le \big\| W_o^{[z]} \big\|_2\, \big\| W_3 \big\|_2\, \big\| W_2 \big\|_2\, \big\| W_1^{[z]} \big\|_2 =: \beta, \qquad \Big\| \frac{\partial \mathbf{z}^e_\tau}{\partial \mathbf{z}^e_s} \Big\|_2 \le \prod_{t=s}^{\tau-1} \big\| P^e_t \big\|_2 \le \beta^{\,\tau - s}, \tag{20.11}\]

where \(W_o^{[z]}\) is the latent rows of \(W_o\) and \(W_1^{[z]}\) the latent columns of \(W_1\) (the latent path ignores every other row and column). This is the vanilla RNN bound \(\sigma_{\max}(W_h)^{T-k}\) of Chapter 222 with one matrix replaced by a product of four (Bengio, Simard, and Frasconi, 1994; Pascanu, Mikolov, and Bengio, 2013). The latent gradient therefore vanishes geometrically whenever the per-year norms stay below one, which happens as soon as the \(\tanh\) units saturate or the CELU sits in its negative range, where its slope \(e^{a/\alpha}\) falls below one. It can explode only if \(\beta > 1\) and the units stay in their linear range. The CELU makes that possible, because it is the identity for positive arguments and a latent coordinate can grow without bound in that range. Over a 20-year horizon a per-year norm of 0.5 leaves \(0.5^{20} \approx 10^{-6}\) of the gradient, and 1.1 multiplies it by \(1.1^{20} \approx 6.7\).

Why the stock path does neither. The accounting carry \(A\) has a simple spectrum. A migrant row \((i, j)\), \(i \neq j\), of \(A\) has only its diagonal entry \(1 - m_j\), and the native row \((j, j)\) has \(1 - m_j + b_j\) on the diagonal plus \(b_j\) in the columns of the migrant cells \((i', j)\) of the same country. Order the stock cells so that the \(n\) native cells come first. Then every off-diagonal entry sits in a native row and a migrant column, above the diagonal, so \(A\) is upper triangular and its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries:

\[ \operatorname{spec}(A) = \{\, 1 - m_j \ (\text{each } n - 1 \text{ times}),\ \ 1 - m_j + b_j \,:\, j = 1, \dots, n \,\}. \tag{20.12}\]

With annual mortality around one percent, every eigenvalue is within about one percent of one: in LSTM language the stock path has a forget gate pinned near 0.99. The learned correction \(M\, \mathrm{diag}(\hat T_t)\, H_t\) has entries of size \(\hat T / \big(s_s (1 + S)\big)\) times a sensitivity \(\partial u / \partial \sigma\), that is, a migration rate of a few percent a year times a number of order one. So \(\partial S_{t+1} / \partial S_t\) stays within a few percent of \(A\) unless the network learns a strongly self-reinforcing response (a larger diaspora raising the flow that feeds it), and only that would make the stock path explode, at a compounding rate of a few percent a year.

Where the paths cross. The two off-diagonal blocks are not alike. The latent-to-stock block carries \(\mathrm{diag}(\hat T_t)\): a latent change moves next year’s stocks through a change in a flow, which is a few percent of a stock per year. The stock-to-latent block carries \(1 / \big(s_s (1 + S)\big)\), so a relative change in a stock enters the latent state at order one. Gradient that starts in the latent state must cross the narrow block to use the stock carry, so the stock path is a poor store for latent memory. Gradient that starts in the stocks enters the latent state cheaply, and from there it is subject to the latent path’s decay over the remaining years. The stock path is also the only channel between edges, since \(P_t\) is block diagonal.

A gated latent update. Chapter 222 shows that the remedy for a vanishing latent path is a gate. Keep the trunk of Equation 20.6 but let the head emit \(1 + 2Z\) numbers, the log flow pre-activation and two vectors \(\mathbf{a}_c, \mathbf{a}_g \in \mathbb{R}^Z\), and replace the latent block by the convex update

\[ \mathbf{z}^e_{t+1} = \mathbf{g} \odot \mathbf{z}^e_t + (1 - \mathbf{g}) \odot \mathbf{c}, \qquad \mathbf{g} = \operatorname{sigmoid}(\mathbf{a}_g), \qquad \mathbf{c} = \tanh(\mathbf{a}_c). \tag{20.13}\]

This is the GRU update of Chapter 222 without the reset gate. The product rule gives the per-edge latent Jacobian

\[ \frac{\partial \mathbf{z}^e_{t+1}}{\partial \mathbf{z}^e_t} = \underbrace{\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{g})}_{\text{direct}} + \mathrm{diag}\big((1 - \mathbf{g}) \odot (1 - \mathbf{c}^2)\big) \frac{\partial \mathbf{a}_c}{\partial \mathbf{z}^e_t} + \mathrm{diag}\big(\mathbf{g} \odot (1 - \mathbf{g}) \odot (\mathbf{z}^e_t - \mathbf{c})\big) \frac{\partial \mathbf{a}_g}{\partial \mathbf{z}^e_t}, \tag{20.14}\]

whose direct term contains no weight matrix: a coordinate whose gate stays near one passes gradient across years whatever the trunk does. The initial gate bias \(b_g\) sets where training starts, \(\mathbf{g} \approx \operatorname{sigmoid}(b_g)\), which is 0.5 per year for \(b_g = 0\) (\(0.5^{20} \approx 10^{-6}\) after 20 years) and 0.88 for \(b_g = 2\) (\(0.88^{20} \approx 0.08\)), the same reasoning as the forget-gate bias of Gers, Schmidhuber, and Cummins (2000) and Jozefowicz, Zaremba, and Sutskever (2015). There is also a closer fit to this particular world. The generator’s memory \(s_j(t) = 0.8\, s_j(t-1) + c_j(t)\) of Section 20.7 is a leaky integrator, and a single gated coordinate with \(g = 0.8\) and candidate \(\kappa\, c_j(t)\), a constant times the conflict indicator, reproduces it exactly up to scale, since \(z_t = 0.8\, z_{t-1} + 0.2\, \kappa\, c_j(t)\) gives \(z_t = 0.2\, \kappa\, s_j(t)\). The ungated block can represent the same trace only through its four-layer composition. The library implements the update and its three local derivatives in gated_latent_update and gated_latent_partials, and the accounting carry in stock_carry_jacobian, in all three languages (Section 20.16).

20.4 Scaling the Data: the Symmetrised Yeo-Johnson Transform

Flows and stocks are heavy tailed: a few corridors carry millions of people and most carry hundreds. A squared-error loss on raw counts would be dominated by the largest corridors and learn nothing about the rest, and net migration has both signs, which rules out a plain logarithm. The paper uses a symmetrised version of the Yeo-Johnson power transform (Yeo and Johnson, 2000):

\[ \varphi_\lambda(x) = \operatorname{sgn}(x) \cdot \begin{cases} \dfrac{(|x| + 1)^{\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} & \lambda \neq 0, \\[2mm] \log(|x| + 1) & \lambda = 0. \end{cases} \tag{20.15}\]

At \(\lambda = 1\) the transform is the identity, as can be checked by substitution; for \(\lambda < 1\) it compresses large magnitudes; at \(\lambda = 0\) it is a signed logarithm. Because the transform is strictly increasing and odd it is invertible, with

\[ \varphi_\lambda^{-1}(y) = \operatorname{sgn}(y) \cdot \begin{cases} \left(1 + \lambda |y|\right)^{1/\lambda} - 1 & \lambda \neq 0, \\ e^{|y|} - 1 & \lambda = 0, \end{cases} \qquad \varphi_\lambda'(x) = (|x| + 1)^{\lambda - 1}. \tag{20.16}\]

The inverse exists only where \(1 + \lambda |y| > 0\), so for negative \(\lambda\) some values of \(y\) have no preimage, and the library raises an error instead of returning nonsense. The derivative is needed later for elasticities. To pick \(\lambda\), one can maximise a Gaussian profile likelihood of the transformed sample, including the Jacobian of the transform:

\[ \ell(\lambda) = -\frac{n}{2} \log \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}\left[\varphi_\lambda(x)\right] + (\lambda - 1) \sum_{m} \log\left(|x_m| + 1\right). \tag{20.17}\]

The paper uses such a maximum likelihood choice to normalise the input covariates and fixes the scaling of the targets at \(\lambda = 0.7\), chosen by a sweep on synthetic data, then standardises the transformed values to zero mean and unit variance. 20.5 The Loss: Match Aggregates, Weight by Trust

With the flow tensor produced by the network, the model can compute stocks, net migration, and total flows, and compare each with its observation. The paper’s loss has the structure of a weighted \(L^2\) loss over all target values,

\[ J = \Big\langle w^{S}_{ij} \big(\varphi_{1}(\Delta \hat S_{ij}) - \varphi_{1}(\Delta S_{ij})\big)^2 \Big\rangle + \Big\langle w^{\mu}_{j} \big(\varphi_{2}(\hat \mu_j) - \varphi_{2}(\mu_j)\big)^2 \Big\rangle + \Big\langle w^{F}_{jk} \big(\varphi_{3}(\hat F_{jk}) - \varphi_{3}(F_{jk})\big)^2 \Big\rangle, \tag{20.18}\]

where angle brackets denote an average over all targets of that type and each \(\varphi_m\) is the transform of Equation 20.15 with standardisation. Two design choices deserve emphasis.

Stock differences, not stocks. The first term compares differences in stock over each five-year interval, not the stocks. Matching stocks directly would force the model’s trajectory to start from a possibly erroneous initial stock and to inherit every census error as a level shift. Differences measure what the flows actually did. The initial level is then recovered after training by a separate least squares step (Section 20.12).

Uncertainty weights. Each target gets a weight built from its relative uncertainty: the inverse of the relative uncertainty, normalised so that the weights have mean one, and clamped to \([0.5, 2]\):

\[ w_k = \operatorname{clip}\!\left( \frac{1 / \sigma_k}{\overline{1/\sigma}},\; 0.5,\; 2 \right). \tag{20.19}\]

Smaller uncertainty gives a larger weight, and the clamp stops one very precise point from dominating and one very noisy point from vanishing. In the paper the Quantmig flows, which come with standard errors, set the weights of the flow targets; other flow targets receive the average Quantmig weight or one; stock weights come from the balancing step below; and net migration targets have weight one. The library implements Equation 20.19 in uncertainty_weights and the loss structure in weighted_l2. Normalising then clamping once can leave the mean slightly different from one; the paper does not spell out whether it renormalises, and the toy does not either.

20.6 Demographic Balancing With Iterative Proportional Fitting

The stock tables themselves are inconsistent: two editions of the same census table do not satisfy the demographic accounting between them. The paper therefore cleans the tables, and uses the amount of cleaning as the uncertainty of each cell. Given stock tables \(S(t_1)\) and \(S(t_2)\), the scheme the paper cites adds the births of the interval to the first table and the deaths to the second, forces both to share the midpoint of their row and column totals, and subtracts births and deaths again. Forcing a table to given margins is done by iterative proportional fitting (IPF; Deming and Stephan, 1940). Alternately rescale each row to its target total and each column to its target total:

\[ x_{ij}^{(2r+1)} = x_{ij}^{(2r)} \frac{R_i}{\sum_{j'} x_{ij'}^{(2r)}}, \qquad x_{ij}^{(2r+2)} = x_{ij}^{(2r+1)} \frac{C_j}{\sum_{i'} x_{i'j}^{(2r+1)}}. \tag{20.20}\]

When the targets have equal totals and the table has a pattern of zeros that permits a solution, the iteration converges to the table with the required margins that is closest to the original in a Kullback-Leibler sense. The code below runs the balancing on the simulated stock tables, which carry log-normal cell noise and a country-level census coverage error, and converts the size of each adjustment into loss weights. The paper’s exact implementation lives in its repository; the function here follows the description in the text and should be read as a faithful reconstruction of the idea, not as a line-by-line copy.

20.7 A Synthetic World With Known Truth

To test whether this machinery recovers anything, we need a world where the truth is known. The generator in make_world builds eight countries and twenty years with these ingredients:

  • Covariates: population, GDP per capita (growing at country-specific rates), pairwise distance, a shared-language indicator, and a conflict indicator.
  • Gravity-style departures. The per-capita rate at which residents of \(j\) move to \(k\) is \(m_{jk} = \exp\{\beta_0 + \beta_g (\log g_k - \log g_j) + \beta_d \log d_{jk} + \beta_l\, \ell_{jk} + \beta_p \log P_k\}\), with \(g\) GDP per capita, \(d\) distance, \(\ell\) common language, \(P\) population, and true coefficients \(\beta_g = 0.8\), \(\beta_d = -0.7\), \(\beta_l = 0.8\), \(\beta_p = 0.5\).
  • A return effect. People return to their country of birth three times as often as the gravity rate alone implies, so the tensor genuinely depends on birth country, and a model for \(F_{jk}\) alone could not generate it.
  • Memory. Two countries suffer conflicts of three years (country 6 in years 6 to 8, country 2 in years 12 and 13). The outflow rate is multiplied by \(1 + 4 s_j(t)\), where \(s_j(t) = 0.8\, s_j(t-1) + c_j(t)\) is an exponentially decaying trace of the conflict indicator \(c_j\). The covariate only shows the three conflict years; the flows stay elevated for years afterwards.
  • Accounting. Flows are \(T_{ijk} = S_{ij}\, m_{jk}\, (1 + 3\,\delta_{ik})\) and stocks evolve by the identity of Equation 20.1 with births and deaths.

Then observe degrades the truth the way the real data are degraded. Stock tables appear every fifth year, off-diagonal only, with log-normal cell noise (5 percent) and a per-table census coverage error for each country of residence (so tables disagree with the accounting identity). Net migration is visible for three countries, and the registry of the second of them counts only 85 percent of true movements. Annual flows are visible for ten corridors, each with its own noise level that is reported as a standard error and used for the weights. Everything else, including the whole birth-country dimension and every other corridor, is hidden.

20.8 Training the Ensemble

The library function train_ensemble assembles the pieces. For each ensemble member it initialises a network with its own random seed and runs the full 20-year rollout from the balanced initial stock table, feeding stocks and latent states back at every step. It forms the three loss terms of Equation 20.18 (stock differences, net migration, corridor flows), each transformed with \(\lambda = 0.7\) and standardised, with uncertainty weights on stocks and flows, and updates the parameters with AdamW and a cosine learning rate schedule. After training, it re-integrates every network from perturbed initial stocks to push the initial-stock uncertainty through (Section 20.13). We train two ensembles on the same data: the recurrent model, and an ablation whose latent state is zeroed every year, which is the toy’s version of the Markovian gravity assumption. The torch implementation is Python only; the numeric pieces it calls are ported to Julia and Rust (Section 20.16).

20.10 Inside the Recurrence: Gradient Paths and a Gated Ablation

Section 20.3.3 makes three predictions about the trained networks. The latent path should lose gradient quickly unless its per-year Jacobian blocks stay near norm one; the stock path should carry gradient across all 20 years almost unchanged; and gradient that starts in the latent state should barely use the stock path, because it must cross the narrow latent-to-stock block. The first experiment measures all three. The second replaces the ungated latent block by the gated update of Equation 20.13 and asks whether that changes what the model recovers.

20.10.1 Measuring the two gradient paths

The cell below rebuilds the rollout of _rollout with two switches. One detaches the stocks fed to the network, so gradient can reach the past only through \(\mathbf{z}\) (the latent path). The other detaches the latent recurrence every year, so gradient can reach the past only through the flows, the accounting identity, and the stock inputs (the stock path). With neither switch the rebuilt rollout is the library’s, which the cell checks first. For each of the five trained recurrent networks it differentiates the mean log flow of the last year, \(\ell = E^{-1} \sum_e u^e_\tau\) with \(\tau = 19\), with respect to the latent state \(\mathbf{z}_s\) and the log stock table \(\log S_s\) of every earlier year, and reports the median over the five networks. The gradient with respect to \(\log S\) is \(S \odot \partial \ell / \partial S\), which puts stocks of very different sizes on a common, relative scale. The mixed curve is the full gradient minus the stock-path gradient, so it collects every path that crosses into the latent state at least once (the latent switch alone gives zero gradient with respect to stocks, by construction). The cell also computes the per-edge latent blocks \(P^e_t\) along each network’s own trajectory, and the learned stock block \(\partial S_{t+1} / \partial S_t\) of Equation 20.10, to compare with the fixed carry \(A\).

Code 
import torch

t0 = time.time()
tau = years - 1  # the last simulated year


def year_step(net, fit, t, stock, z, cut_stock=False):
    # One year of the library rollout: network output and next year's stocks.
    norm = fit["norm"]
    I, J, K = (torch.as_tensor(a) for a in fit["edge"])
    src = stock.detach() if cut_stock else stock
    s_ij = (torch.log1p(src[I, J].clamp(min=0.0)) - norm["s_mu"]) / norm["s_sd"]
    s_ik = (torch.log1p(src[I, K].clamp(min=0.0)) - norm["s_mu"]) / norm["s_sd"]
    out = net(torch.cat([fit["static"][t], s_ij[:, None], s_ik[:, None], z], dim=1))
    T = torch.zeros(n, n, n).index_put((I, J, K), torch.exp(out[:, 0]))
    births = torch.tensor(world.birth_rate, dtype=torch.float32) * stock.sum(0)
    mort = torch.tensor(world.mortality, dtype=torch.float32)
    return out, stock + torch.diag(births) - mort[None, :] * stock + T.sum(1) - T.sum(2)


def rollout_paths(net, fit, mode="full"):
    # Keeps every state and can cut one recurrent path. "full": both paths.
    # "latent": the stocks fed to the network are detached, so gradient reaches the
    # past only through z. "stock": the latent recurrence is detached every year, so
    # gradient travels only through the flows, the accounting identity and the stock
    # inputs.
    stock = torch.tensor(np.maximum(fit["stock0"], 0.0), dtype=torch.float32, requires_grad=True)
    z = torch.zeros(len(fit["edge"][0]), fit["cfg"].latent, requires_grad=True)
    zs, ss, us = [z], [stock], []
    for t in range(years):
        out, stock = year_step(net, fit, t, stock, z, cut_stock=(mode == "latent"))
        if mode == "stock":
            z = out[:, 1:].detach().requires_grad_(True)
        else:
            z = out[:, 1:]
            z.retain_grad()
        stock.retain_grad()
        us.append(out[:, 0])
        zs.append(z)
        ss.append(stock)
    return us, zs, ss

    # Gradient of the mean log flow in the last year with respect to z(s) (all edges)
    # and to log S(s), for s = 0..tau, as vectors, one list per ensemble member.
    gz, gs = [], []
    for net in fit["nets"]:
        us, zs, ss = rollout_paths(net, fit, mode)
        us[tau].mean().backward()
        gz.append([zs[s].grad.flatten() if zs[s].grad is not None else torch.zeros(zs[s].numel())
                   for s in range(tau + 1)])
        gs.append([(ss[s].grad * ss[s].detach()).flatten() if ss[s].grad is not None
                   else torch.zeros(n * n) for s in range(tau + 1)])
    return gz, gs


def by_lag(vecs):
    # median over members of the gradient norm, indexed by lag tau - s
    return np.median([[float(v[tau - L].norm()) for L in range(tau + 1)] for v in vecs], axis=0)

def latent_carry(fit):
    # One-year latent Jacobian blocks P_t = dz(t+1)/dz(t), one Z x Z block per edge,
    # along each member's own trajectory, and the spectral norm of their products.
    one_year, prod_norms = [], []
    for net in fit["nets"]:
        trace = []
        with torch.no_grad():
            mf._rollout(torch, net, fit["static"], torch.tensor(np.maximum(fit["stock0"], 0.0),
                        dtype=torch.float32), world, fit["cfg"], fit["norm"], years, fit["edge"], trace=trace)
        Z = fit["cfg"].latent
        prod = torch.eye(Z).expand(len(fit["edge"][0]), Z, Z)
        norms_t = [torch.ones(len(fit["edge"][0]))]
        for feats, zt in trace:
            x = torch.cat([feats, zt], dim=1).requires_grad_(True)
            out = net(x)
            rows = [torch.autograd.grad(out[:, 1 + a].sum(), x, retain_graph=True)[0][:, -Z:]
                    for a in range(Z)]
            P = torch.stack(rows, dim=1)  # (E, Z, Z)
            one_year.append(torch.linalg.matrix_norm(P, ord=2))
            prod = P @ prod
            norms_t.append(torch.linalg.matrix_norm(prod, ord=2))
        prod_norms.append(torch.stack(norms_t).numpy())  # (years + 1, E)
    return torch.cat(one_year).numpy(), np.concatenate(prod_norms, axis=1)

def latent_bound(net, Z):
    # beta of the latent-path bound: latent rows of W_o, W_3, W_2, latent columns of W_1
    W = [m_.weight.detach() for m_ in net if isinstance(m_, torch.nn.Linear)]
    parts = [W[0][:, -Z:], *W[1:-1], W[-1][1:]]
    return float(np.prod([torch.linalg.matrix_norm(w, ord=2).item() for w in parts]))

The gradient norm summed over all edges decays more slowly, from 0.0282 at lag 0 to 0.0006 at lag 19 along the latent path alone, a factor of about 44, because a sum of norms is dominated by the few edges whose per-year norm stays near or above one. The stock path neither vanishes nor explodes. Along the stock path alone the gradient with respect to \(\log S_s\) is flat, 0.0351 at lag 0 and 0.0381 at lag 19. The crossing is asymmetric. Gradient that starts in the latent state almost never uses the stock carry: through the stock path alone the gradient with respect to \(\mathbf{z}_s\) is 0.0002 at lag 1, against 0.0158 through the latent path. Gradient that starts in the stocks crosses easily: the mixed paths grow with the lag and, at lag 19, the full gradient with respect to \(\log S_s\) (0.0816) is 2.1 times the stock path’s alone. Every extra year of lag adds another year in which a stock perturbation can enter the latent state and reach \(\ell\) through a short latent segment at the end.

In plain terms, for most edges the trained Elman block remembers a shock for a few years at most, and the stock path, which does remember, can store a latent memory only by writing it into flows, a channel too narrow to hold it. That matches what Figure 20.6 showed for country 2, whose recovered outflow decays too fast after the conflict.

20.11 Elasticities Through the Chain Rule

A flow estimate is useful, but a researcher also wants to know what drives it. Because the model is differentiable, the sensitivity of the log flow to any covariate is available by automatic differentiation. The paper defines the elasticity of the flow with respect to the \(i\)-th covariate as

\[ \varepsilon_i = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_i} |x_i| = \frac{\partial u}{\partial \tilde{x}_i} \, \varphi_{\lambda}'(x_i)\, |x_i|, \qquad \tilde x_i = \varphi_\lambda(x_i), \tag{20.21}\]

with \(u = \log \hat T\). The network differentiates with respect to the transformed input \(\tilde x_i\), and the chain rule through Equation 20.16 converts that to a response to the raw covariate. Reading \(\varepsilon_i\): a one percent increase in the covariate changes the flow by about \(\varepsilon_i\) percent. The library function elasticity_chain does the conversion and feature_elasticity differentiates the trained networks with respect to GDP per capita of the origin and the destination. In the toy ground truth the answers are known: \(+\beta_g = +0.8\) for the destination and \(-0.8\) for the origin.

The recurrent ensemble’s origin elasticity is essentially zero (truth \(-0.8\)) and its destination elasticity has the right sign but about a fifth of the true size (truth \(+0.8\)); the memoryless ensemble is no better. The chain rule is implemented correctly (the unit tests check the conversion), but a derivative of a flexible network is only as good as the identification the data provide. The paper’s setting, with 230 countries, 34 years, and 18 covariates, gives the network far more variation to separate the effects, and Gaskin and Abel use the elasticities for interpretation only after the network has been validated on held-out data. The lesson for readers building such models is to test elasticity recovery on a synthetic truth before interpreting elasticities on real data.

20.12 Calibrating the Initial Stock

Because the loss matches stock differences, the trained model fixes only changes in stocks, not their level. To produce a stock time series \(\hat S_{ij}(t)\) one needs an initial condition, and the paper computes the best constant offset after training instead of trusting a noisy first census. Let \(\tilde s_j(t) = \prod_{t_0 \le \tau < t} (1 - m_j(\tau))\) be the survival fraction of the initial cohort in country \(j\). Shifting the initial stock by \(b_{ij}\) shifts the modelled stock at \(t\) by \(\tilde s_j(t)\, b_{ij}\), so the weighted least squares problem has a closed form:

\[ b_{ij} = \frac{\sum_{t} \tilde s_j(t)\, w_{ij}(t) \left( S_{ij}(t) - \hat S_{ij}(t) \right)}{\sum_{t} w_{ij}(t)\, \tilde s_j(t)^2}, \tag{20.22}\]

with \(b_{ij}\) raised, if necessary, to the smallest value that keeps every shifted stock non-negative. The function calibrate_offset implements Equation 20.22 including the constraint, and survival_fraction builds \(\tilde s_j\). The cell below integrates the ensemble mean flows from the observed initial table, fits the offsets, and measures the stock error against the true stocks before and after.

20.13 Uncertainty: an Ensemble Plus a Pushforward

Gaskin and Abel separate two sources of uncertainty. The first is ill-posedness: many parameter settings fit the data about equally well, but may imply different flows. They sample this by training an ensemble of networks from different random initialisations, which approximates a distribution over the parameters (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel, and Blundell, 2017). The second is input uncertainty: the covariates themselves are noisy. Given a distribution \(\rho_0\) over the inputs, the distribution of the output is the pushforward of \(\rho_0\) through the trained map,

\[ p(\hat T) = \left( u_{\#} \rho_0 \right)(\hat T). \tag{20.23}\]

In the paper the only input uncertainty that is quantified is the initial stock (obtained from the demographic balancing), so each of the 15 trained networks is run on 100 samples of the initial stocks, giving \(15 \times 100 = 1500\) samples of the flow tensor, of which the published dataset reports the mean and one standard deviation. train_ensemble does the same at toy scale: 5 networks times 10 draws gives 50 samples, with each initial cell perturbed by a log-normal factor whose scale is the cell’s balancing uncertainty. The next cell separates the two sources in the toy, to see how much each contributes to the total spread.

In the toy the initial-stock pushforward contributes almost nothing (0.0003 against 0.0729 for the disagreement between members), because the initial-stock uncertainties produced by the balancing are small and the flows depend on the stock only through a log-scale input feature. Almost all of the spread comes from the ensemble, that is, from the ill-posedness of the problem. That ordering need not carry over to the real data, where the initial stocks are far noisier, and it is a reason to keep both sources in the pipeline.

  1. Download from the Zenodo record the migration estimates and the training inputs. Check the record’s file listing and licence first, and record the checksum of what you download.
  2. Reproduce a known number before trusting anything. The paper reports that global annual movements rose from about 13 million in 2000 to over 36 million in 2023, and that the largest single-year flow in its estimates is Venezuela to Colombia in 2019 at roughly 850,000 people. Aggregating the published flow table and matching these two figures is a cheap test that you have read the file correctly.
  3. Run the accounting checks of this chapter on the real table. Using od_flows and net_migration on any year of the published tensor, the net migration over all countries must sum to zero, and stock_step applied to a year’s stocks, births, mortality rates, and flows must reproduce the next year’s stocks to rounding. A table that fails these tests has been read with the wrong axis order or units.
  4. Compare with held-out sources. The paper validates against flows withheld from training. If you have a national series that was not used (the paper lists the net migration sources by country in its supplement), compare it with the net migration implied by the estimates, using the metrics of Section 20.9.

20.15 Alternative Viewpoints and Limits

  • Identifiability is the central worry. Estimating \(T_{ijk}\) from aggregates is ill-posed, and the network’s smoothness and the covariates are doing real work. If the covariates say Syrians are unlikely to move from Turkey to Germany, the output will reflect that prior. The ensemble spread does not account for such prior-driven error, as the toy shows (Section 20.9). Independent validation data, not model fit, is the evidence.
  • The covariates are themselves estimates. GDP, trade, and refugee stock series have gaps that the paper fills by extrapolation. Errors in them propagate unmodelled, apart from the initial stocks.
  • The loss design embeds judgement. Net migration targets get weight one; the source priority order (Quantmig, then national statistics, then Facebook) decides which dataset wins in an overlap; and the clamp range is a hyperparameter. A different but defensible choice changes the answer for badly measured corridors.
  • Definitions of “a move” differ. The paper restricts to sources with a one-year definition, but even those are not identical. A neural network that matches several definitions at once learns a compromise, not a definition.
  • Simpler baselines deserve a fair trial. The paper compares with stock differencing and demographic accounting. A reader should also try a regularised gravity model plus the accounting identity, with a latent state added only if it wins, to know what the neural network is buying.
  • The same pattern applies elsewhere. Hidden-state estimation from several aggregates shows up wherever a flow is unobservable but conserved: epidemiology (infections from reported cases, hospitalisations, and deaths), supply chains (shipments from inventories and sales), and finance (flows from balance sheets). The ingredients are the same: write the identity, parameterise the unknown with a covariate-driven network, give it a memory, weight sources by trust, and report an ensemble.

20.17 Summary

The paper’s contribution is a way of posing the estimation problem as much as a model. The flow tensor \(T_{ijk}\) is never observed, but its aggregates (stocks, net migration, corridor totals) are, by instruments that disagree. Writing the exact accounting identity of Equation 20.1 inside a recurrent network lets one training run reconcile all of them, with the network’s covariate-driven smoothness selecting one solution among the many consistent ones. Four supporting choices make it work: the symmetrised Yeo-Johnson transform so heavy-tailed and signed values can share a loss; matching differences of stocks so census level errors do not leak in; weights built from the uncertainty of each source; and an ensemble with an initial-stock pushforward to put an interval on the answer.

The synthetic replication shows the idea working and shows its limits. The accounting identity holds to rounding error, the recurrent ensemble fits its targets much better than a memoryless one, it orders corridors it never saw correctly, and it reproduces the slow decay after a shock where a memoryless model cannot. It also shows what the method does not give for free: unobserved small corridors are underestimated by a sizeable margin, the ensemble band is too narrow because it cannot see bias shared by all members, and elasticities are not recovered when the covariates vary too little to identify them. Looking inside the recurrence adds a further lesson. The model has two recurrent paths: an ungated Elman latent block, whose gradient vanishes within a few years for most edges, and the accounting identity, an additive carry with a forget gate fixed near 0.99 by mortality. Replacing the latent block by a gated, GRU-style update keeps the latent gradient alive across all 20 years and, in this run, improves the fit and the post-shock recovery. These are the reasons the paper validates on held-out real flows, and the reasons a reader adopting the pattern for a new problem should build a synthetic world first and measure what comes back.

# Learning Four Decades of Migration: A Stock-Flow Model Inside a Neural Network {#sec-migration-flows}

::: {.chapter-subtitle}
How Gaskin and Abel (2026) estimate bilateral flows nobody measured, by embedding an accounting identity in a recurrent network, and a small replication on a synthetic world
:::

Nobody counts how many people move from one country to another. Some countries register arrivals and departures, most do not, and the ones that do use different definitions of a "move". Border crossings count tourists. Population registers count only people who bother to deregister. Censuses count how many foreign-born people live in a country on one day every five or ten years, which says almost nothing about how many arrived and left in between. Yet researchers, governments, and humanitarian agencies need annual, bilateral, country-of-birth-resolved flows, because that is the quantity that responds to wars, recessions, and visa rules.

In 2026, Thomas Gaskin and Guy Abel published an answer in *Nature* (Gaskin and Abel, 2026): a dataset of origin-destination migration flows and stocks between 230 countries and regions, disaggregated by country of birth, from 1990 onward, with uncertainty bounds, produced by a deep recurrent neural network. This chapter is about *how the problem was set up*, because the setup is the transferable lesson. The authors did not find a labelled dataset of true flows and fit a regression to it, because no such dataset exists. They wrote down the one fact that is certain (people are conserved: a stock changes only through births, deaths, and moves), built that accounting identity into the training loop of a neural network, and asked the network to find the unobserved flow tensor that makes every partial, noisy, mutually inconsistent observation come out approximately right. The method belongs to the family called scientific machine learning or differentiable physics (Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis, 2019; Rackauckas et al., 2020), applied to a social system instead of a physical one.

::: {.callout-important appearance="simple"}
## The thesis of this chapter in one paragraph

When the quantity you need is never observed directly, but several aggregates of it are observed by different noisy instruments, do not discard the instruments that disagree. Write the exact relation between the hidden quantity and each aggregate, put those relations inside a differentiable model, and train the model on the aggregates. Add three ingredients that make this work in practice: a recurrent latent state so that today's flows can depend on the whole past (@sec-mf-network), a variable transform and an uncertainty weighting so that no single huge value or unreliable source dominates the loss (@sec-mf-scaling and @sec-mf-loss), and an ensemble so that the answer comes with an honest spread (@sec-mf-uncertainty). The chapter derives each piece, implements it in a tested tri-language library, and replicates the idea on a synthetic world of eight countries where the truth is known, so that we can measure what the method recovers and where it fails.
:::

The structure is as follows. We first state what the paper observed and what it estimated (@sec-mf-problem), derive the accounting identity and count unknowns against observations (@sec-mf-accounting), describe the recurrent network, why memory matters, how it relates to the RNN, LSTM, and GRU, and how gradients travel through its two recurrent paths (@sec-mf-network, @sec-mf-rnn-family, @sec-mf-bptt), then the scaling, loss, and demographic balancing (@sec-mf-scaling, @sec-mf-loss, @sec-mf-balancing). A synthetic world follows (@sec-mf-world), with training and an honest recovery check (@sec-mf-training and @sec-mf-results), and a measurement of the trained model's gradient paths with a gated ablation (@sec-mf-recurrence-experiments). We then cover elasticities, initial-stock calibration, and uncertainty (@sec-mf-elasticity, @sec-mf-calibration, @sec-mf-uncertainty), what to change to rerun on the real data (@sec-mf-real), and the reference implementation in Python, Julia, and Rust (@sec-mf-reference-implementation).

## The Problem: Four Instruments, None of Them the Quantity {#sec-mf-problem}

Index countries by $i$ (country of birth), $j$ (country of origin of a move, meaning where the person lived before moving), and $k$ (destination). The object of interest is the **flow tensor** $T_{ijk}(t)$, the number of people born in $i$ who moved from $j$ to $k$ in year $t$. Setting $i = j$ gives moves by natives of $j$ leaving home; $i = k$ gives people returning to their country of birth; $i$ distinct from both gives onward migration, such as a person born in Syria moving from Turkey to Germany. The aggregates the world actually publishes are sums of this tensor:

* **Migrant stocks** $S_{ij}(t)$: the number of people born in $i$ living in $j$ at time $t$. The UN Department of Economic and Social Affairs publishes these every five years, with revisions that do not agree across editions.
* **Total flows** $F_{jk}(t) = \sum_i T_{ijk}(t)$ for a few corridors: harmonised intra-European estimates (the paper uses the Quantmig database, which comes with uncertainty), national statistics from a few countries, and Facebook-derived estimates for 2019 to 2022.
* **Net migration** $\mu_j(t)$ for about 30 countries from national statistical offices. The UN's own net migration series is computed as a demographic residual (population change minus births plus deaths), so the paper does not train on it, and instead uses it as a comparison.

Each instrument measures a different slice with a different definition, and the paper's first figure shows that even for a single corridor the sending country's deregistrations and the receiving country's registrations differ by large factors. The estimation problem is therefore not "denoise one dataset". It is **reconcile several datasets that were never meant to agree, through a latent object that explains all of them**.

## The Accounting Identity and the Counting Problem {#sec-mf-accounting}

The one thing we know for certain is that people are conserved. Let $B_j(t)$ be births in country $j$ and $\mu_j^{\text{d}}(t)$ the mortality rate there (written $m_j$ below to keep $\mu$ free for net migration). The stock of people born in $i$ living in $j$ evolves as

$$
\frac{\partial S_{ij}}{\partial t} = \delta_{ij} B_j - m_j S_{ij} + \sum_{k} \left( T_{ikj} - T_{ijk} \right),
$$ {#eq-mf-stock}

where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta. The three terms say, in order: every birth in $j$ adds to the native-born stock $S_{jj}$; deaths remove a fraction of every stock; and the third term sums arrivals of $i$-born people into $j$ from every other country $k$ minus departures from $j$ to every $k$. Aggregating the tensor gives the quantities that are actually observed,

$$
F_{jk} = \sum_i T_{ijk}, \qquad \mu_j = \sum_k F_{kj} - \sum_k F_{jk}.
$$ {#eq-mf-aggregates}

In discrete annual form, which is what the code integrates, $S_{ij}(t+1) = S_{ij}(t) + \delta_{ij} B_j - m_j S_{ij}(t) + \sum_k (T_{ikj} - T_{ijk})$. The function `stock_step` implements exactly this, `od_flows` computes $F$, and `net_migration` computes $\mu$.

**Conservation.** Summing @eq-mf-stock over all $i$ and $j$, the migration term telescopes: each flow $T_{ijk}$ appears once with a plus sign (arrival in $k$) and once with a minus sign (departure from $j$), so

$$
\sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial S_{ij}}{\partial t} = \sum_j B_j - \sum_j m_j P_j, \qquad P_j = \sum_i S_{ij},
$$ {#eq-mf-conserve}

and likewise $\sum_j \mu_j = 0$. Migration moves people between cells but never creates or destroys them. This is not a modelling assumption that data could contradict. It is an identity, and it is what makes training possible: any flow tensor the network proposes automatically implies a full set of stocks, a full set of net migration figures, and a full set of corridor flows that are consistent with one another, even though the *observed* versions of those quantities are not.

The next cell checks the identity on the synthetic world built later in the chapter, and counts unknowns against observations. The counting is the heart of why the problem is hard.

```{python}
#| label: cell-mf-setup
```

The toy has `{python} n_stock + n_net + n_flow` observations for `{python} f"{unknowns:,}"` unknowns, about `{python} f"{(n_stock + n_net + n_flow) / unknowns:.2f}"` per unknown, and the paper's problem is far more underdetermined, because the number of unknowns grows as the cube of the number of countries while observations grow roughly with the square. The system of equations has many solutions. What selects among them is the other ingredient: the network ties the flows of different edges and different years together through shared parameters and shared covariates, so that the answer must be a *smooth function of covariates* rather than an arbitrary table. The accounting identity supplies the constraints; the network supplies the inductive bias. Gaskin and Abel are explicit that the problem is ill-posed in this sense and use an ensemble precisely to measure how much the answer depends on which of the many fitting solutions the optimiser lands on.

Classical methods choose different inductive biases. **Stock differencing** sets the flow from $i$ to $j$ to the positive part of $S_{ij}(t + 5) - S_{ij}(t)$ divided by five (Abel, 2013). It assumes people move only between their birth country and a destination, ignores births and deaths, drops negative changes, and can only produce five-year averages. **Demographic accounting** methods estimate a three-dimensional flow table subject to the stock differences (Abel, 2013; Azose and Raftery, 2019; Abel and Cohen, 2019), but treat stocks as exact and use no covariates, so they cannot produce annual flows or explain them. **Gravity models** regress flows on covariates and can in principle give annual flows, but the paper argues they treat people as Markovian, reacting only to this year's covariates, which is hard to defend for long-run economic and political conditions. The recurrent network is the paper's attempt to keep the covariates of the gravity approach and the exactness of the accounting approach while adding memory.

## The Recurrent Network: Flows With Memory {#sec-mf-network}

The paper's mapping takes the covariates of the three countries involved, $\boldsymbol{\phi}_{ijk}(t)$, together with a hidden state $\mathbf{z}_{ijk}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{Z}$, and returns a log flow estimate and the next hidden state:

$$
u_\theta\!\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{ijk}(t), \mathbf{z}_{ijk}(t)\right) = \left( \log \hat T_{ijk}(t),\; \mathbf{z}_{ijk}(t+1) \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 + Z}.
$$ {#eq-mf-recurrence}

The latent state starts at zero and "is a memory of past changes and their effects on the present flow estimate". The paper cites the encoder-decoder papers of Cho et al. (2014) and Sutskever, Vinyals, and Le (2014) for its recurrent network, but the mapping in @eq-mf-recurrence has no gates and no separate memory cell, so it is not a long short-term memory network (LSTM; Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) or a gated recurrent unit (GRU). It is a *simple recurrent network* in the sense of Elman (1990): part of the network's activity at year $t$ is copied back and read as an extra input at year $t + 1$, and the same network is applied independently to every edge $(i, j, k)$, with the recurrence running over years. @sec-mf-rnn-family places it precisely among the vanilla RNN, the LSTM, and the GRU. The covariates include the migrant stocks $S_{ij}$ and $S_{ik}$, which are not observed every year. In intermediate years the stocks fed to the network come from the network's own earlier predictions, via the accounting identity. That is a second recursion: output at year $t$ determines stocks at $t + 1$, which are inputs at $t + 1$.

Why bother with memory? The paper's argument is that the decision to leave is "not merely predicated on the current economic climate: crises from past years can influence a person's decision". There are mechanical reasons too: diaspora networks, remittances, and visa regimes all carry the past forward. A feedforward network that maps this year's covariates to this year's flows has no way to represent a shock that raised flows two years ago and is still decaying. The toy builds this effect into the ground truth on purpose, so that we can test whether the latent state captures it (@sec-mf-results).

Two details matter for implementation. First, the output is $\log \hat T$, so $\hat T = \exp(\cdot) > 0$ and the model cannot propose negative flows; the paper finishes the network with a CELU activation (Barron, 2017), which is linear for positive inputs. With a positive scale $\alpha$, as in the toy's $\alpha = 8$, it saturates smoothly at $-\alpha$ below, giving a floor on $\log T$. Second, training runs through the whole simulated history, so gradients flow backward through every year of the recursion (backpropagation through time; Werbos, 1990), and the full loss is computed on aggregates over all edges. In the toy the whole 20-year rollout for all 448 edges is one forward pass, so this is cheap. At the paper's scale it is the reason training is a serious computational project. @sec-mf-bptt works out which paths that gradient takes and when it vanishes.

The code below shows the model the chapter's library trains: an MLP with tanh hidden layers and a CELU output, applied to every edge, with the latent block of the output fed back as input. The network is deliberately small. Nothing in the method depends on size, and a small network makes the replication fast and the failure modes visible.

### The recurrence the library implements {#sec-mf-rnn-equations}

Write $e = (i, j, k)$ for an edge, $E = n^2 (n - 1)$ for the number of edges (448 in the toy), $\boldsymbol{\phi}^e_t \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$ for the edge's standardised covariates in year $t$ (populations and GDP of origin and destination, distance, common language, the two conflict indicators, and the two native indicators $\delta_{ij}$, $\delta_{ik}$), and $Z$ for the latent width. The network sees the two stock cells of an edge through a logarithm and a standardisation,

$$
\sigma(S) = \frac{\log\big(1 + \max(S, 0)\big) - \mu_s}{s_s},
$$ {#eq-mf-sigma}

where $\mu_s$ and $s_s$ are the mean and standard deviation of the log observed stocks. One year of the model for edge $e$ is then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{x}^e_t &= \big[\boldsymbol{\phi}^e_t;\ \sigma(S_{ij,t});\ \sigma(S_{ik,t});\ \mathbf{z}^e_t\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{12 + Z}, \\
\mathbf{h}^{(0)} &= \mathbf{x}^e_t, \qquad \mathbf{h}^{(l)} = \tanh\big(W_l \mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}_l\big), \quad l = 1, \dots, L, \\
\mathbf{o}^e_t &= \operatorname{CELU}_\alpha\big(W_o \mathbf{h}^{(L)} + \mathbf{b}_o\big) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 + Z}, \\
u^e_t &= \log \hat T^e_t = o^e_{t,0}, \qquad \mathbf{z}^e_{t+1} = \big(o^e_{t,1}, \dots, o^e_{t,Z}\big), \qquad \mathbf{z}^e_0 = \mathbf{0},
\end{aligned}
$$ {#eq-mf-rnn-step}

with $\operatorname{CELU}_\alpha(a) = \max(0, a) + \min\big(0, \alpha(e^{a/\alpha} - 1)\big)$ applied coordinate by coordinate. The toy uses $L = 3$ layers of 32 units, $Z = 8$, and $\alpha = 8$ (`TrainConfig`); the paper uses 7 layers of 60 units and $Z = 100$. The flows close the loop through the accounting identity, with births proportional to the population of the country of residence, $B_j = b_j \sum_{i'} S_{i'j}$:

$$
S_{t+1} = A(S_t) + R(\hat T_t), \qquad
\big[A(S)\big]_{ij} = (1 - m_j)\, S_{ij} + \delta_{ij}\, b_j \sum_{i'} S_{i'j}, \qquad
\big[R(T)\big]_{ij} = \sum_k T_{ikj} - \sum_k T_{ijk}.
$$ {#eq-mf-rnn-stock}

Two features of these equations decide everything that follows. First, the latent block is read off the *output layer* and copied back into the input layer one year later. Copied-back units are Elman's context units, and the only difference from his network is what sits between the copy and its next value: Elman's network has one hidden layer, so a context vector passes through one affine map and one squashing function per step, while here $\mathbf{z}$ passes through $L + 1 = 4$ affine maps, three $\tanh$ layers, and a CELU. Pascanu et al. (2014) call this a *deep transition* recurrent network. The vanilla RNN of @sec-road-to-transformers is the special case $L = 0$, with $\tanh$ in place of the CELU. Second, the network's state is not all overwritten each year. The latent coordinates are, but the stocks are carried forward by a fixed affine map $A$, and only the increment $R(\hat T_t)$ is produced by the network.

### Where it sits among the RNN, the LSTM, and the GRU {#sec-mf-rnn-family}

@tbl-266-rnn-cells in @sec-road-to-transformers compares the three classical recurrent cells by the state they carry, how they update it, and the direct term of the Jacobian of that update, which controls how gradient travels backward through time. The same three questions applied to this chapter's model give @tbl-mf-rnn-family. The model carries two states at once, and they answer the questions differently.

| | Vanilla RNN (Elman, 1990) | This model: latent path | This model: stock path | LSTM | GRU |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| State carried | $h_t$ | $\mathbf{z}^e_t$, private to edge $e$ | $S_t$, shared by all edges | $(h_t, c_t)$ | $h_t$ |
| How it is updated | overwritten, $\tanh(W_h h + W_x x + b)$ | overwritten, CELU of a four-layer network | added, $A(S_t) + R(\hat T_t)$ | added, $f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde c_t$ | interpolated, $g_t \odot h_{t-1} + (1 - g_t) \odot \tilde h_t$ |
| Direct term of the carry Jacobian | $\mathrm{diag}(1 - h_t^2)\, W_h$ | a product of four weight matrices and four activation derivatives | $A$, eigenvalues $1 - m_j$ and $1 - m_j + b_j$ | $\mathrm{diag}(f_t)$ | $\mathrm{diag}(g_t)$ |
| Who sets the carry | weights | weights | demography, fixed | learned forget gate | learned update gate |
| What writes into it | the whole update | the whole update | the network's flows | input gate times candidate | one minus update gate, times candidate |
| Bounded | yes, in $(-1, 1)$ | below only, $z > -\alpha$ | no | no, $c_t$ is unbounded | yes, in $(-1, 1)$ |

: The two recurrent paths of the migration model next to the classical cells. The latent path is an ungated deep-transition Elman recurrence; the stock path is an additive carry whose "forget gate" is fixed by mortality and whose write is the network's flow output. The GRU's update gate, written $z_t$ in the Road to Transformers chapter, is written $g_t$ here because $\mathbf{z}$ is the latent state. {#tbl-mf-rnn-family}

Read as a whole, the network is an Elman recurrence in its latent block with something like an LSTM cell running beside it. The stock table plays the part of the LSTM's cell state $c_t$: it is carried forward additively, with a coefficient $1 - m_j \approx 0.99$ where the LSTM has its forget gate $f_t$, and the flows play the part of the gated write $i_t \odot \tilde c_t$. The difference is who controls the carry. An LSTM learns $f_t$ and can choose what to keep; here demography sets the carry, and the network can use it only indirectly, by writing flows whose effect on the stocks it later reads back through @eq-mf-sigma. Note also that the "no memory" ablation of @sec-mf-training zeroes $\mathbf{z}$ every year but keeps the stock path, so it is memoryless only in its latent block.

### Backpropagation through time along two paths {#sec-mf-bptt}

Training minimises the loss of @eq-mf-loss, which depends on the flows of every year, by differentiating through the whole 20-year rollout (backpropagation through time; Werbos, 1990). Stack the state of year $t$ as $X_t = (\mathbf{z}_t, S_t)$, where $\mathbf{z}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{EZ}$ collects the latent states of all edges and $S_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ is the stock table. @eq-mf-rnn-step and @eq-mf-rnn-stock define a map $X_{t+1} = \Phi_t(X_t)$. For a loss term $\ell$ that depends on the log flows $\mathbf{u}_\tau$ of a late year $\tau$, the chain rule gives its gradient with respect to the state of an earlier year $s < \tau$ as

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial X_s} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mathbf{u}_\tau}\, \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}_\tau}{\partial X_\tau}\, \mathcal{J}_{\tau-1}\, \mathcal{J}_{\tau-2} \cdots \mathcal{J}_s, \qquad \mathcal{J}_t = \frac{\partial X_{t+1}}{\partial X_t}.
$$ {#eq-mf-bptt-chain}

**The one-year Jacobian of the network.** For one edge, differentiate @eq-mf-rnn-step layer by layer. Each layer contributes its weight matrix and the derivative of its activation, $\tanh'(a) = 1 - \tanh^2(a)$, and for the output layer

$$
\operatorname{CELU}'_\alpha(a) = \begin{cases} 1 & a > 0, \\ e^{a/\alpha} & a \le 0, \end{cases}
$$

so the Jacobian of the output with respect to the input is the product

$$
D^e_t = \frac{\partial \mathbf{o}^e_t}{\partial \mathbf{x}^e_t} = \mathrm{diag}\big(\operatorname{CELU}'_\alpha(\mathbf{a}_o)\big)\, W_o\, \mathrm{diag}\big(1 - (\mathbf{h}^{(L)})^2\big)\, W_L \cdots \mathrm{diag}\big(1 - (\mathbf{h}^{(1)})^2\big)\, W_1,
$$ {#eq-mf-rnn-jacobian}

with $\mathbf{a}_o = W_o \mathbf{h}^{(L)} + \mathbf{b}_o$. Its rows split into the flow (row 0) and the latent block (rows 1 to $Z$), and its columns into the covariates, the two stock inputs, and the latent inputs. That defines four per-edge blocks: $P^e_t = \partial \mathbf{z}^e_{t+1} / \partial \mathbf{z}^e_t$ ($Z \times Z$), $\mathbf{g}^e_t = \partial u^e_t / \partial \mathbf{z}^e_t$ ($1 \times Z$), and the derivatives of $\mathbf{z}^e_{t+1}$ and $u^e_t$ with respect to the two stock inputs, which pick up the factor $\partial \sigma / \partial S = 1 / \big(s_s (1 + S)\big)$ from @eq-mf-sigma.

**The one-year Jacobian of the state.** Collect the per-edge blocks over all edges: $P_t = \mathrm{blockdiag}_e(P^e_t)$ of size $EZ \times EZ$, $G_t = \mathrm{blockdiag}_e(\mathbf{g}^e_t)$ of size $E \times EZ$, $Q_t = \partial \mathbf{z}_{t+1} / \partial S_t$ of size $EZ \times n^2$, and $H_t = \partial \mathbf{u}_t / \partial S_t$ of size $E \times n^2$. The flow is $\hat T = e^{u}$, so $\partial \hat T / \partial u = \mathrm{diag}(\hat T)$, and $R$ is linear, $R(\hat T) = M \hat T$, where column $e = (i, j, k)$ of $M \in \mathbb{R}^{n^2 \times E}$ has $+1$ in row $(i, k)$ (an arrival) and $-1$ in row $(i, j)$ (a departure). Writing $A$ also for the $n^2 \times n^2$ matrix of the linear map in @eq-mf-rnn-stock, differentiating $X_{t+1} = \Phi_t(X_t)$ block by block gives

$$
\mathcal{J}_t =
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{z}_{t+1}}{\partial \mathbf{z}_t} & \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{z}_{t+1}}{\partial S_t} \\[3mm]
\dfrac{\partial S_{t+1}}{\partial \mathbf{z}_t} & \dfrac{\partial S_{t+1}}{\partial S_t}
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
P_t & Q_t \\
M\, \mathrm{diag}(\hat T_t)\, G_t & A + M\, \mathrm{diag}(\hat T_t)\, H_t
\end{pmatrix}.
$$ {#eq-mf-bptt-blocks}

Multiplying out @eq-mf-bptt-chain, every term is a path through these blocks. A path that stays in the top-left block is the *latent path*, one that stays in the bottom-right block is the *stock path*, and a path that switches between them is *mixed*. @fig-mf-two-paths draws one year.

```{mermaid}

flowchart LR

    S0["stocks S at t"] -->|"A plus MTH: stock path"| S1["stocks S at t plus 1"]
    S0 -->|"Q: stock inputs"| Z1
    Z0 -->|"MTG: flows"| S1

    S0 --> U
```

**When the latent path vanishes or explodes.** $P_t$ is block diagonal, so each edge's latent memory is private: it never mixes with another edge's latent state. Because $0 < \tanh' \le 1$ and $0 < \operatorname{CELU}'_\alpha \le 1$, the diagonal factors in @eq-mf-rnn-jacobian have spectral norm at most one, and

$$
\big\| P^e_t \big\|_2 \le \big\| W_o^{[z]} \big\|_2\, \big\| W_3 \big\|_2\, \big\| W_2 \big\|_2\, \big\| W_1^{[z]} \big\|_2 =: \beta,
\qquad
\Big\| \frac{\partial \mathbf{z}^e_\tau}{\partial \mathbf{z}^e_s} \Big\|_2 \le \prod_{t=s}^{\tau-1} \big\| P^e_t \big\|_2 \le \beta^{\,\tau - s},
$$ {#eq-mf-bptt-latent}

where $W_o^{[z]}$ is the latent rows of $W_o$ and $W_1^{[z]}$ the latent columns of $W_1$ (the latent path ignores every other row and column). This is the vanilla RNN bound $\sigma_{\max}(W_h)^{T-k}$ of @sec-road-to-transformers with one matrix replaced by a product of four (Bengio, Simard, and Frasconi, 1994; Pascanu, Mikolov, and Bengio, 2013). The latent gradient therefore vanishes geometrically whenever the per-year norms stay below one, which happens as soon as the $\tanh$ units saturate or the CELU sits in its negative range, where its slope $e^{a/\alpha}$ falls below one. It can explode only if $\beta > 1$ *and* the units stay in their linear range. The CELU makes that possible, because it is the identity for positive arguments and a latent coordinate can grow without bound in that range. Over a 20-year horizon a per-year norm of 0.5 leaves $0.5^{20} \approx 10^{-6}$ of the gradient, and 1.1 multiplies it by $1.1^{20} \approx 6.7$.

**Why the stock path does neither.** The accounting carry $A$ has a simple spectrum. A migrant row $(i, j)$, $i \neq j$, of $A$ has only its diagonal entry $1 - m_j$, and the native row $(j, j)$ has $1 - m_j + b_j$ on the diagonal plus $b_j$ in the columns of the migrant cells $(i', j)$ of the same country. Order the stock cells so that the $n$ native cells come first. Then every off-diagonal entry sits in a native row and a migrant column, above the diagonal, so $A$ is upper triangular and its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries:

$$
\operatorname{spec}(A) = \{\, 1 - m_j \ (\text{each } n - 1 \text{ times}),\ \ 1 - m_j + b_j \,:\, j = 1, \dots, n \,\}.
$$ {#eq-mf-carry-spectrum}

With annual mortality around one percent, every eigenvalue is within about one percent of one: in LSTM language the stock path has a forget gate pinned near 0.99. The learned correction $M\, \mathrm{diag}(\hat T_t)\, H_t$ has entries of size $\hat T / \big(s_s (1 + S)\big)$ times a sensitivity $\partial u / \partial \sigma$, that is, a migration rate of a few percent a year times a number of order one. So $\partial S_{t+1} / \partial S_t$ stays within a few percent of $A$ unless the network learns a strongly self-reinforcing response (a larger diaspora raising the flow that feeds it), and only that would make the stock path explode, at a compounding rate of a few percent a year.

**Where the paths cross.** The two off-diagonal blocks are not alike. The latent-to-stock block carries $\mathrm{diag}(\hat T_t)$: a latent change moves next year's stocks through a change in a flow, which is a few percent of a stock per year. The stock-to-latent block carries $1 / \big(s_s (1 + S)\big)$, so a *relative* change in a stock enters the latent state at order one. Gradient that starts in the latent state must cross the narrow block to use the stock carry, so the stock path is a poor store for latent memory. Gradient that starts in the stocks enters the latent state cheaply, and from there it is subject to the latent path's decay over the remaining years. The stock path is also the only channel between edges, since $P_t$ is block diagonal.

**A gated latent update.** @sec-road-to-transformers shows that the remedy for a vanishing latent path is a gate. Keep the trunk of @eq-mf-rnn-step but let the head emit $1 + 2Z$ numbers, the log flow pre-activation and two vectors $\mathbf{a}_c, \mathbf{a}_g \in \mathbb{R}^Z$, and replace the latent block by the convex update

$$
\mathbf{z}^e_{t+1} = \mathbf{g} \odot \mathbf{z}^e_t + (1 - \mathbf{g}) \odot \mathbf{c}, \qquad \mathbf{g} = \operatorname{sigmoid}(\mathbf{a}_g), \qquad \mathbf{c} = \tanh(\mathbf{a}_c).
$$ {#eq-mf-gated}

This is the GRU update of @sec-road-to-transformers without the reset gate. The product rule gives the per-edge latent Jacobian

$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{z}^e_{t+1}}{\partial \mathbf{z}^e_t} = \underbrace{\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{g})}_{\text{direct}} + \mathrm{diag}\big((1 - \mathbf{g}) \odot (1 - \mathbf{c}^2)\big) \frac{\partial \mathbf{a}_c}{\partial \mathbf{z}^e_t} + \mathrm{diag}\big(\mathbf{g} \odot (1 - \mathbf{g}) \odot (\mathbf{z}^e_t - \mathbf{c})\big) \frac{\partial \mathbf{a}_g}{\partial \mathbf{z}^e_t},
$$ {#eq-mf-gated-jacobian}

whose direct term contains no weight matrix: a coordinate whose gate stays near one passes gradient across years whatever the trunk does. The initial gate bias $b_g$ sets where training starts, $\mathbf{g} \approx \operatorname{sigmoid}(b_g)$, which is 0.5 per year for $b_g = 0$ ($0.5^{20} \approx 10^{-6}$ after 20 years) and 0.88 for $b_g = 2$ ($0.88^{20} \approx 0.08$), the same reasoning as the forget-gate bias of Gers, Schmidhuber, and Cummins (2000) and Jozefowicz, Zaremba, and Sutskever (2015). There is also a closer fit to this particular world. The generator's memory $s_j(t) = 0.8\, s_j(t-1) + c_j(t)$ of @sec-mf-world is a leaky integrator, and a single gated coordinate with $g = 0.8$ and candidate $\kappa\, c_j(t)$, a constant times the conflict indicator, reproduces it exactly up to scale, since $z_t = 0.8\, z_{t-1} + 0.2\, \kappa\, c_j(t)$ gives $z_t = 0.2\, \kappa\, s_j(t)$. The ungated block can represent the same trace only through its four-layer composition. The library implements the update and its three local derivatives in `gated_latent_update` and `gated_latent_partials`, and the accounting carry in `stock_carry_jacobian`, in all three languages (@sec-mf-reference-implementation).

The cell below checks the derivations symbolically: the block structure of @eq-mf-bptt-blocks on a scalar model with one latent unit and one stock cell, the gated Jacobian @eq-mf-gated-jacobian, and the factorisation of the characteristic polynomial of $A$ behind @eq-mf-carry-spectrum. It then evaluates the carry of the synthetic world.

```{python}
#| label: cell-mf-bptt-check
import sympy as sp

# (1) A scalar caricature of the two recurrent paths: one latent unit z and one stock
#     cell S that receives the flow. The network reads log(1 + S), as the library does.
z_s, S_s, w_z, w_s, v_z, v_s, m_s = sp.symbols("z S w_z w_s v_z v_s m", real=True)
z_next = sp.tanh(w_z * z_s + w_s * sp.log(1 + S_s))       # latent path
u = v_z * z_s + v_s * sp.log(1 + S_s)                     # log flow
S_next = (1 - m_s) * S_s + sp.exp(u)                      # accounting carry plus arrivals
J_auto = sp.Matrix([z_next, S_next]).jacobian([z_s, S_s])
P_ = (1 - z_next**2) * w_z                                # d z' / d z
Q_ = (1 - z_next**2) * w_s / (1 + S_s)                    # d z' / d S
G_, H_, T_ = v_z, v_s / (1 + S_s), sp.exp(u)              # d u / d z, d u / d S, the flow
J_derived = sp.Matrix([[P_, Q_], [T_ * G_, (1 - m_s) + T_ * H_]])
print("SymPy: block Jacobian of the scalar model minus the derived blocks:",
      list(sp.simplify(J_auto - J_derived)))

# (2) The gated update: direct carry diag(g) plus the candidate and gate terms.
w_c, w_g, x_c, x_g = sp.symbols("w_c w_g x_c x_g", real=True)
g_s = 1 / (1 + sp.exp(-(w_g * z_s + x_g)))
c_s = sp.tanh(w_c * z_s + x_c)
z_gated = g_s * z_s + (1 - g_s) * c_s
dz_derived = g_s + (1 - g_s) * (1 - c_s**2) * w_c + g_s * (1 - g_s) * (z_s - c_s) * w_g
print("SymPy: d z'/d z of the gated update minus the derived Jacobian:",
      sp.simplify(sp.diff(z_gated, z_s) - dz_derived))

# (3) The accounting carry A for two countries: its characteristic polynomial factors
#     into (lambda - (1 - m_j)) and (lambda - (1 - m_j + b_j)) for each country j.
lam = sp.symbols("lambda")
bs, ms = sp.symbols("b_1 b_2", positive=True), sp.symbols("m_1 m_2", positive=True)
A_sym = sp.zeros(4, 4)
for i in range(2):
    for j in range(2):
        A_sym[i * 2 + j, i * 2 + j] = 1 - ms[j]
for j in range(2):
    for i2 in range(2):
        A_sym[j * 2 + j, i2 * 2 + j] += bs[j]
target = sp.prod([(lam - (1 - ms[j])) * (lam - (1 - ms[j] + bs[j])) for j in range(2)])
print("SymPy: char poly of A minus the product of its claimed factors:",
      sp.expand(A_sym.charpoly(lam).as_expr() - target))
num = {bs[0]: 0.012, bs[1]: 0.01, ms[0]: 0.01, ms[1]: 0.008}
print("library stock_carry_jacobian equals the symbolic A:",
      bool(np.allclose(np.array(A_sym.subs(num), dtype=float),
                       mf.stock_carry_jacobian([0.012, 0.01], [0.01, 0.008]))))

# (4) The accounting carry of the synthetic world, over the 20-year horizon.
A_world = mf.stock_carry_jacobian(world.birth_rate, world.mortality)
eig_A = np.linalg.eigvals(A_world).real
surv20 = (1 - world.mortality) ** years
grow20 = (1 - world.mortality + world.birth_rate) ** years
normA20 = float(np.linalg.norm(np.linalg.matrix_power(A_world, years), 2))
print(f"\neigenvalues of the accounting carry A: {eig_A.min():.4f} to {eig_A.max():.4f}")
print(f"over {years} years: migrant cells keep (1 - m)^{years} = {surv20.min():.3f} to {surv20.max():.3f}, "
      f"native cells grow by at most {grow20.max():.3f}, and ||A^{years}||_2 = {normA20:.3f}")
```

All three symbolic differences are zero. For the synthetic world the eigenvalues of $A$ lie between `{python} f"{eig_A.min():.4f}"` and `{python} f"{eig_A.max():.4f}"`, so over the 20 simulated years a migrant cell keeps between `{python} f"{surv20.min():.3f}"` and `{python} f"{surv20.max():.3f}"` of a perturbation and a native cell amplifies it by at most `{python} f"{grow20.max():.3f}"`. The spectral norm of $A^{20}$ is somewhat larger, `{python} f"{normA20:.3f}"`, because $A$ is not a normal matrix: births couple every cell of a column to its native cell, and a perturbation can grow transiently along that coupling even though no eigenvalue exceeds `{python} f"{eig_A.max():.4f}"`. Neither number is anywhere near vanishing or exploding. @sec-mf-recurrence-experiments measures all three paths in the trained networks.

## Scaling the Data: the Symmetrised Yeo-Johnson Transform {#sec-mf-scaling}

Flows and stocks are heavy tailed: a few corridors carry millions of people and most carry hundreds. A squared-error loss on raw counts would be dominated by the largest corridors and learn nothing about the rest, and net migration has both signs, which rules out a plain logarithm. The paper uses a symmetrised version of the Yeo-Johnson power transform (Yeo and Johnson, 2000):

$$
\varphi_\lambda(x) = \operatorname{sgn}(x) \cdot
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{(|x| + 1)^{\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} & \lambda \neq 0, \\[2mm]
\log(|x| + 1) & \lambda = 0.
\end{cases}
$$ {#eq-mf-yj}

At $\lambda = 1$ the transform is the identity, as can be checked by substitution; for $\lambda < 1$ it compresses large magnitudes; at $\lambda = 0$ it is a signed logarithm. Because the transform is strictly increasing and odd it is invertible, with

$$
\varphi_\lambda^{-1}(y) = \operatorname{sgn}(y) \cdot
\begin{cases}
\left(1 + \lambda |y|\right)^{1/\lambda} - 1 & \lambda \neq 0, \\
e^{|y|} - 1 & \lambda = 0,
\end{cases}
\qquad
\varphi_\lambda'(x) = (|x| + 1)^{\lambda - 1}.
$$ {#eq-mf-yj-inverse}

The inverse exists only where $1 + \lambda |y| > 0$, so for negative $\lambda$ some values of $y$ have no preimage, and the library raises an error instead of returning nonsense. The derivative is needed later for elasticities. To pick $\lambda$, one can maximise a Gaussian profile likelihood of the transformed sample, including the Jacobian of the transform:

$$
\ell(\lambda) = -\frac{n}{2} \log \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}\left[\varphi_\lambda(x)\right] + (\lambda - 1) \sum_{m} \log\left(|x_m| + 1\right).
$$ {#eq-mf-yj-loglik}

The paper uses such a maximum likelihood choice to normalise the input covariates and fixes the scaling of the targets at $\lambda = 0.7$, chosen by a sweep on synthetic data, then standardises the transformed values to zero mean and unit variance. The cell below shows the transform on a skewed sample and verifies the round trip.

```{python}

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
```

## The Loss: Match Aggregates, Weight by Trust {#sec-mf-loss}

With the flow tensor produced by the network, the model can compute stocks, net migration, and total flows, and compare each with its observation. The paper's loss has the structure of a weighted $L^2$ loss over all target values,

$$
J = \Big\langle w^{S}_{ij} \big(\varphi_{1}(\Delta \hat S_{ij}) - \varphi_{1}(\Delta S_{ij})\big)^2 \Big\rangle
+ \Big\langle w^{\mu}_{j} \big(\varphi_{2}(\hat \mu_j) - \varphi_{2}(\mu_j)\big)^2 \Big\rangle
+ \Big\langle w^{F}_{jk} \big(\varphi_{3}(\hat F_{jk}) - \varphi_{3}(F_{jk})\big)^2 \Big\rangle,
$$ {#eq-mf-loss}

where angle brackets denote an average over all targets of that type and each $\varphi_m$ is the transform of @eq-mf-yj with standardisation. Two design choices deserve emphasis.

**Stock differences, not stocks.** The first term compares *differences* in stock over each five-year interval, not the stocks. Matching stocks directly would force the model's trajectory to start from a possibly erroneous initial stock and to inherit every census error as a level shift. Differences measure what the flows actually did. The initial level is then recovered after training by a separate least squares step (@sec-mf-calibration).

**Uncertainty weights.** Each target gets a weight built from its relative uncertainty: the inverse of the relative uncertainty, normalised so that the weights have mean one, and clamped to $[0.5, 2]$:

$$
w_k = \operatorname{clip}\!\left( \frac{1 / \sigma_k}{\overline{1/\sigma}},\; 0.5,\; 2 \right).
$$ {#eq-mf-weights}

Smaller uncertainty gives a larger weight, and the clamp stops one very precise point from dominating and one very noisy point from vanishing. In the paper the Quantmig flows, which come with standard errors, set the weights of the flow targets; other flow targets receive the average Quantmig weight or one; stock weights come from the balancing step below; and net migration targets have weight one. The library implements @eq-mf-weights in `uncertainty_weights` and the loss structure in `weighted_l2`. Normalising then clamping once can leave the mean slightly different from one; the paper does not spell out whether it renormalises, and the toy does not either.

## Demographic Balancing With Iterative Proportional Fitting {#sec-mf-balancing}

The stock tables themselves are inconsistent: two editions of the same census table do not satisfy the demographic accounting between them. The paper therefore cleans the tables, and uses the amount of cleaning as the uncertainty of each cell. Given stock tables $S(t_1)$ and $S(t_2)$, the scheme the paper cites adds the births of the interval to the first table and the deaths to the second, forces both to share the *midpoint* of their row and column totals, and subtracts births and deaths again. Forcing a table to given margins is done by **iterative proportional fitting** (IPF; Deming and Stephan, 1940). Alternately rescale each row to its target total and each column to its target total:

$$
x_{ij}^{(2r+1)} = x_{ij}^{(2r)} \frac{R_i}{\sum_{j'} x_{ij'}^{(2r)}}, \qquad
x_{ij}^{(2r+2)} = x_{ij}^{(2r+1)} \frac{C_j}{\sum_{i'} x_{i'j}^{(2r+1)}}.
$$ {#eq-mf-ipf}

When the targets have equal totals and the table has a pattern of zeros that permits a solution, the iteration converges to the table with the required margins that is closest to the original in a Kullback-Leibler sense. The code below runs the balancing on the simulated stock tables, which carry log-normal cell noise and a country-level census coverage error, and converts the size of each adjustment into loss weights. The paper's exact implementation lives in its repository; the function here follows the description in the text and should be read as a faithful reconstruction of the idea, not as a line-by-line copy.

```{python}
#| label: fig-mf-balance
#| fig-cap: "Demographic balancing of the simulated stock tables. Left: relative adjustment made to each off-diagonal cell by iterative proportional fitting, for the first five-year interval. Right: the loss weights that result from converting those adjustments to uncertainties; the clamp at 0.5 and 2 is visible as spikes."

ax[1].hist(w_stock, bins=25, color="tab:orange"); ax[1].set_xlabel("loss weight")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
print(f"mean relative error of raw stock cells against the truth:      {true_rel.mean():.3f}")
print(f"mean relative error of balanced stock cells against the truth: {bal_rel.mean():.3f}")
print(f"weights at the lower clamp: {np.mean(w_stock <= 0.5 + 1e-9):.0%}, upper clamp: {np.mean(w_stock >= 2 - 1e-9):.0%}")
```

The printed comparison is a useful reality check, and in this toy it is not flattering. Against the known truth the balanced cells (mean relative error `{python} f"{bal_rel.mean():.3f}"`) are *not* closer than the raw cells (`{python} f"{true_rel.mean():.3f}"`). The reason is structural: the margins that IPF enforces include the native stock, which the generator fills in as population minus the noisy migrant cells, so the margins carry the same noise as the cells and the fit enforces mutual consistency, not accuracy. Balancing is therefore best read as a measure of how much the tables disagree with demographic accounting, which is exactly how the paper uses it (the size of the adjustment becomes the cell's uncertainty), and not as a denoiser. The heavy tail of that disagreement also means most weights sit on a clamp (`{python} f"{np.mean(w_stock <= 0.5 + 1e-9):.0%}"` at the lower bound), so the clamp range is doing a lot of the work. On the real data nobody can compare against the truth, which is why the paper validates the final flows instead (@sec-mf-real).

## A Synthetic World With Known Truth {#sec-mf-world}

To test whether this machinery recovers anything, we need a world where the truth is known. The generator in `make_world` builds eight countries and twenty years with these ingredients:

* **Covariates:** population, GDP per capita (growing at country-specific rates), pairwise distance, a shared-language indicator, and a conflict indicator.
* **Gravity-style departures.** The per-capita rate at which residents of $j$ move to $k$ is $m_{jk} = \exp\{\beta_0 + \beta_g (\log g_k - \log g_j) + \beta_d \log d_{jk} + \beta_l\, \ell_{jk} + \beta_p \log P_k\}$, with $g$ GDP per capita, $d$ distance, $\ell$ common language, $P$ population, and true coefficients $\beta_g = 0.8$, $\beta_d = -0.7$, $\beta_l = 0.8$, $\beta_p = 0.5$.
* **A return effect.** People return to their country of birth three times as often as the gravity rate alone implies, so the tensor genuinely depends on birth country, and a model for $F_{jk}$ alone could not generate it.
* **Memory.** Two countries suffer conflicts of three years (country 6 in years 6 to 8, country 2 in years 12 and 13). The outflow rate is multiplied by $1 + 4 s_j(t)$, where $s_j(t) = 0.8\, s_j(t-1) + c_j(t)$ is an exponentially decaying trace of the conflict indicator $c_j$. The *covariate* only shows the three conflict years; the *flows* stay elevated for years afterwards.
* **Accounting.** Flows are $T_{ijk} = S_{ij}\, m_{jk}\, (1 + 3\,\delta_{ik})$ and stocks evolve by the identity of @eq-mf-stock with births and deaths.

Then `observe` degrades the truth the way the real data are degraded. Stock tables appear every fifth year, off-diagonal only, with log-normal cell noise (5 percent) and a per-table census coverage error for each country of residence (so tables disagree with the accounting identity). Net migration is visible for three countries, and the registry of the second of them counts only 85 percent of true movements. Annual flows are visible for ten corridors, each with its own noise level that is reported as a standard error and used for the weights. Everything else, including the whole birth-country dimension and every other corridor, is hidden.

## Training the Ensemble {#sec-mf-training}

The library function `train_ensemble` assembles the pieces. For each ensemble member it initialises a network with its own random seed and runs the full 20-year rollout from the balanced initial stock table, feeding stocks and latent states back at every step. It forms the three loss terms of @eq-mf-loss (stock differences, net migration, corridor flows), each transformed with $\lambda = 0.7$ and standardised, with uncertainty weights on stocks and flows, and updates the parameters with AdamW and a cosine learning rate schedule. After training, it re-integrates every network from perturbed initial stocks to push the initial-stock uncertainty through (@sec-mf-uncertainty). We train two ensembles on the same data: the recurrent model, and an ablation whose latent state is zeroed every year, which is the toy's version of the Markovian gravity assumption. The torch implementation is Python only; the numeric pieces it calls are ported to Julia and Rust (@sec-mf-reference-implementation).

1. **Structure is recovered much better than level.** The log correlation is high, but the mean log ratio of estimate to truth is `{python} f"{ratio.mean():+.2f}"`, a bias of `{python} f"{np.exp(ratio.mean()) - 1:+.0%}"`, and the scatter plot shows the points mostly below the diagonal at all sizes, with the largest corridors underestimated by a factor of about two. ## Inside the Recurrence: Gradient Paths and a Gated Ablation {#sec-mf-recurrence-experiments}

@sec-mf-bptt makes three predictions about the trained networks. The latent path should lose gradient quickly unless its per-year Jacobian blocks stay near norm one; the stock path should carry gradient across all 20 years almost unchanged; and gradient that starts in the latent state should barely use the stock path, because it must cross the narrow latent-to-stock block. The first experiment measures all three. The second replaces the ungated latent block by the gated update of @eq-mf-gated and asks whether that changes what the model recovers.

### Measuring the two gradient paths

The cell below rebuilds the rollout of `_rollout` with two switches. One detaches the stocks fed to the network, so gradient can reach the past only through $\mathbf{z}$ (the latent path). The other detaches the latent recurrence every year, so gradient can reach the past only through the flows, the accounting identity, and the stock inputs (the stock path). With neither switch the rebuilt rollout is the library's, which the cell checks first. For each of the five trained recurrent networks it differentiates the mean log flow of the last year, $\ell = E^{-1} \sum_e u^e_\tau$ with $\tau = 19$, with respect to the latent state $\mathbf{z}_s$ and the log stock table $\log S_s$ of every earlier year, and reports the median over the five networks. The gradient with respect to $\log S$ is $S \odot \partial \ell / \partial S$, which puts stocks of very different sizes on a common, relative scale. The *mixed* curve is the full gradient minus the stock-path gradient, so it collects every path that crosses into the latent state at least once (the latent switch alone gives zero gradient with respect to stocks, by construction). The cell also computes the per-edge latent blocks $P^e_t$ along each network's own trajectory, and the learned stock block $\partial S_{t+1} / \partial S_t$ of @eq-mf-bptt-blocks, to compare with the fixed carry $A$.

```{python}
#| label: fig-mf-gradient-paths
#| fig-cap: "Gradient paths through the trained recurrent ensemble (median over five networks). Left: norm of the gradient of the last year's mean log flow with respect to the latent state s years earlier, through both paths, the latent path alone, and the stock path alone. Middle: the same with respect to the log stock table, split into the stock path and the mixed paths that cross into the latent state. Right: spectral norm of the k-year Jacobian of the latent path for the median and the 90th percentile edge, against the accounting carry A to the power k."
import torch

t0 = time.time()
tau = years - 1  # the last simulated year


def year_step(net, fit, t, stock, z, cut_stock=False):
    # One year of the library rollout: network output and next year's stocks.
    norm = fit["norm"]
    I, J, K = (torch.as_tensor(a) for a in fit["edge"])
    src = stock.detach() if cut_stock else stock
    s_ij = (torch.log1p(src[I, J].clamp(min=0.0)) - norm["s_mu"]) / norm["s_sd"]
    s_ik = (torch.log1p(src[I, K].clamp(min=0.0)) - norm["s_mu"]) / norm["s_sd"]
    out = net(torch.cat([fit["static"][t], s_ij[:, None], s_ik[:, None], z], dim=1))
    T = torch.zeros(n, n, n).index_put((I, J, K), torch.exp(out[:, 0]))
    births = torch.tensor(world.birth_rate, dtype=torch.float32) * stock.sum(0)
    mort = torch.tensor(world.mortality, dtype=torch.float32)
    return out, stock + torch.diag(births) - mort[None, :] * stock + T.sum(1) - T.sum(2)


def rollout_paths(net, fit, mode="full"):
    # Keeps every state and can cut one recurrent path. "full": both paths.
    # "latent": the stocks fed to the network are detached, so gradient reaches the
    # past only through z. "stock": the latent recurrence is detached every year, so
    # gradient travels only through the flows, the accounting identity and the stock
    # inputs.
    stock = torch.tensor(np.maximum(fit["stock0"], 0.0), dtype=torch.float32, requires_grad=True)
    z = torch.zeros(len(fit["edge"][0]), fit["cfg"].latent, requires_grad=True)
    zs, ss, us = [z], [stock], []
    for t in range(years):
        out, stock = year_step(net, fit, t, stock, z, cut_stock=(mode == "latent"))
        if mode == "stock":
            z = out[:, 1:].detach().requires_grad_(True)
        else:
            z = out[:, 1:]
            z.retain_grad()
        stock.retain_grad()
        us.append(out[:, 0])
        zs.append(z)
        ss.append(stock)
    return us, zs, ss


# The replica reproduces the library's rollout.
with torch.no_grad():
    f_lib, _ = mf._rollout(torch, fit_mem["nets"][0], fit_mem["static"],
                           torch.tensor(np.maximum(fit_mem["stock0"], 0.0), dtype=torch.float32),
                           world, fit_mem["cfg"], fit_mem["norm"], years, fit_mem["edge"])
us0, _, _ = rollout_paths(fit_mem["nets"][0], fit_mem)
I_, J_, K_ = fit_mem["edge"]
replica_err = float((torch.stack(us0).detach() - torch.log(f_lib[:, I_, J_, K_])).abs().max())
print(f"replica against the library rollout, max |log T difference|: {replica_err:.1e}")


def path_gradients(fit, mode):
    # Gradient of the mean log flow in the last year with respect to z(s) (all edges)
    # and to log S(s), for s = 0..tau, as vectors, one list per ensemble member.
    gz, gs = [], []
    for net in fit["nets"]:
        us, zs, ss = rollout_paths(net, fit, mode)
        us[tau].mean().backward()
        gz.append([zs[s].grad.flatten() if zs[s].grad is not None else torch.zeros(zs[s].numel())
                   for s in range(tau + 1)])
        gs.append([(ss[s].grad * ss[s].detach()).flatten() if ss[s].grad is not None
                   else torch.zeros(n * n) for s in range(tau + 1)])
    return gz, gs


def by_lag(vecs):
    # median over members of the gradient norm, indexed by lag tau - s
    return np.median([[float(v[tau - L].norm()) for L in range(tau + 1)] for v in vecs], axis=0)


paths = {mode: path_gradients(fit_mem, mode) for mode in ("full", "latent", "stock")}
mixed_s = [[paths["full"][1][q][s] - paths["stock"][1][q][s] for s in range(tau + 1)]
           for q in range(len(fit_mem["nets"]))]
grad_z = {mode: by_lag(paths[mode][0]) for mode in paths}
grad_s = {mode: by_lag(paths[mode][1]) for mode in ("full", "stock")}
grad_s["mixed"] = by_lag(mixed_s)


def latent_carry(fit):
    # One-year latent Jacobian blocks P_t = dz(t+1)/dz(t), one Z x Z block per edge,
    # along each member's own trajectory, and the spectral norm of their products.
    one_year, prod_norms = [], []
    for net in fit["nets"]:
        trace = []
        with torch.no_grad():
            mf._rollout(torch, net, fit["static"], torch.tensor(np.maximum(fit["stock0"], 0.0),
                        dtype=torch.float32), world, fit["cfg"], fit["norm"], years, fit["edge"], trace=trace)
        Z = fit["cfg"].latent
        prod = torch.eye(Z).expand(len(fit["edge"][0]), Z, Z)
        norms_t = [torch.ones(len(fit["edge"][0]))]
        for feats, zt in trace:
            x = torch.cat([feats, zt], dim=1).requires_grad_(True)
            out = net(x)
            rows = [torch.autograd.grad(out[:, 1 + a].sum(), x, retain_graph=True)[0][:, -Z:]
                    for a in range(Z)]
            P = torch.stack(rows, dim=1)  # (E, Z, Z)
            one_year.append(torch.linalg.matrix_norm(P, ord=2))
            prod = P @ prod
            norms_t.append(torch.linalg.matrix_norm(prod, ord=2))
        prod_norms.append(torch.stack(norms_t).numpy())  # (years + 1, E)
    return torch.cat(one_year).numpy(), np.concatenate(prod_norms, axis=1)


P1_mem, Pprod_mem = latent_carry(fit_mem)


def latent_bound(net, Z):
    # beta of the latent-path bound: latent rows of W_o, W_3, W_2, latent columns of W_1
    W = [m_.weight.detach() for m_ in net if isinstance(m_, torch.nn.Linear)]
    parts = [W[0][:, -Z:], *W[1:-1], W[-1][1:]]
    return float(np.prod([torch.linalg.matrix_norm(w, ord=2).item() for w in parts]))


beta_mem = np.array([latent_bound(net, fit_mem["cfg"].latent) for net in fit_mem["nets"]])


def learned_stock_block(fit):
    # J_SS(t) = A + M diag(T) H_t, the stock block of the trained model in each year,
    # with the latent state held at its value on the trajectory.
    rho, dev, prod_norm = [], [], []
    for net in fit["nets"]:

                                    dtype=torch.float32), world, fit["cfg"], fit["norm"], years, fit["edge"],

        prod = np.eye(n * n)
        for t in range(years):

            Jm = torch.autograd.functional.jacobian(step, stocks[t], vectorize=True)
            Jm = Jm.reshape(n * n, n * n).double().numpy()
            rho.append(np.abs(np.linalg.eigvals(Jm)).max())
            dev.append(np.linalg.norm(Jm - A_world, 2))
            prod = Jm @ prod
        prod_norm.append(np.linalg.norm(prod, 2))
    return np.array(rho), np.array(dev), np.array(prod_norm)
```

In plain terms, for most edges the trained Elman block remembers a shock for a few years at most, and the stock path, which does remember, can store a latent memory only by writing it into flows, a channel too narrow to hold it. ### A gated latent update

`TrainConfig(cell="gated")` keeps the inputs, the $\tanh$ trunk, the CELU flow output, the loss, the seeds, and the training schedule of the recurrent ensemble, and replaces only the latent block by @eq-mf-gated. The head of the network emits $1 + 2Z$ pre-activations instead of $1 + Z$, the latent block of the output is `gated_latent_update` applied to the input's latent block, and `gate_bias` sets the initial update-gate bias $b_g$. We train two ensembles: $b_g = 0$, which starts every gate at 0.5, and $b_g = 2$, which starts them at 0.88 so that the network remembers by default.

## Elasticities Through the Chain Rule {#sec-mf-elasticity}

A flow estimate is useful, but a researcher also wants to know what *drives* it. Because the model is differentiable, the sensitivity of the log flow to any covariate is available by automatic differentiation. The paper defines the elasticity of the flow with respect to the $i$-th covariate as

$$
\varepsilon_i = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_i} |x_i| = \frac{\partial u}{\partial \tilde{x}_i} \, \varphi_{\lambda}'(x_i)\, |x_i|, \qquad \tilde x_i = \varphi_\lambda(x_i),
$$ {#eq-mf-elasticity}

with $u = \log \hat T$. The network differentiates with respect to the transformed input $\tilde x_i$, and the chain rule through @eq-mf-yj-inverse converts that to a response to the raw covariate. Reading $\varepsilon_i$: a one percent increase in the covariate changes the flow by about $\varepsilon_i$ percent. The library function `elasticity_chain` does the conversion and `feature_elasticity` differentiates the trained networks with respect to GDP per capita of the origin and the destination. In the toy ground truth the answers are known: $+\beta_g = +0.8$ for the destination and $-0.8$ for the origin.

This is a negative result worth keeping. With eight countries, GDP varies in the cross-section across only eight values and drifts slowly in time, so the data cannot separate the GDP effect from the other covariates. The recurrent ensemble's origin elasticity is essentially zero (truth $-0.8$) and its destination elasticity has the right sign but about a fifth of the true size (truth $+0.8$); the memoryless ensemble is no better. The chain rule is implemented correctly (the unit tests check the conversion), but a derivative of a flexible network is only as good as the identification the data provide. The paper's setting, with 230 countries, 34 years, and 18 covariates, gives the network far more variation to separate the effects, and Gaskin and Abel use the elasticities for interpretation only after the network has been validated on held-out data. The lesson for readers building such models is to test elasticity recovery on a synthetic truth *before* interpreting elasticities on real data.

## Calibrating the Initial Stock {#sec-mf-calibration}

Because the loss matches stock differences, the trained model fixes only changes in stocks, not their level. To produce a stock *time series* $\hat S_{ij}(t)$ one needs an initial condition, and the paper computes the best constant offset after training instead of trusting a noisy first census. Let $\tilde s_j(t) = \prod_{t_0 \le \tau < t} (1 - m_j(\tau))$ be the survival fraction of the initial cohort in country $j$. Shifting the initial stock by $b_{ij}$ shifts the modelled stock at $t$ by $\tilde s_j(t)\, b_{ij}$, so the weighted least squares problem has a closed form:

$$
b_{ij} = \frac{\sum_{t} \tilde s_j(t)\, w_{ij}(t) \left( S_{ij}(t) - \hat S_{ij}(t) \right)}{\sum_{t} w_{ij}(t)\, \tilde s_j(t)^2},
$$ {#eq-mf-offset}

with $b_{ij}$ raised, if necessary, to the smallest value that keeps every shifted stock non-negative. The function `calibrate_offset` implements @eq-mf-offset including the constraint, and `survival_fraction` builds $\tilde s_j$. The cell below integrates the ensemble mean flows from the observed initial table, fits the offsets, and measures the stock error against the true stocks before and after.

```{python}
print(f"{'integrated from the observed first table':44s}{rmse(s_hat, all_years):12.1f}{rmse(s_hat, st):14.1f}")
print(f"{'after the offset calibration':44s}{rmse(shifted, all_years):12.1f}{rmse(shifted, st):14.1f}")
print(f"{'raw observed tables (census years only)':44s}{'':>12s}{rmse_raw:14.1f}")
```

The offset calibration reduces the stock error (from `{python} f"{rmse(s_hat, all_years):.0f}"` to `{python} f"{rmse(shifted, all_years):.0f}"` thousand over all years), as it should, since it is the least squares fit to the observed tables. But at the census years the raw tables, which are the truth plus 5 percent noise, are closer to the truth (`{python} f"{rmse_raw:.0f}"` thousand) than the model's calibrated series (`{python} f"{rmse(shifted, st):.0f}"`). That is the expected ordering: the model was trained on stock *changes* and flows, never on stock levels, so it is not a good stock reconstruction. What the model adds is the years between censuses, where the raw data have nothing at all, and the decomposition of changes into flows.

## Uncertainty: an Ensemble Plus a Pushforward {#sec-mf-uncertainty}

Gaskin and Abel separate two sources of uncertainty. The first is **ill-posedness**: many parameter settings fit the data about equally well, but may imply different flows. They sample this by training an *ensemble* of networks from different random initialisations, which approximates a distribution over the parameters (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel, and Blundell, 2017). The second is **input uncertainty**: the covariates themselves are noisy. Given a distribution $\rho_0$ over the inputs, the distribution of the output is the pushforward of $\rho_0$ through the trained map,

$$
p(\hat T) = \left( u_{\#} \rho_0 \right)(\hat T).
$$ {#eq-mf-pushforward}

In the paper the only input uncertainty that is quantified is the initial stock (obtained from the demographic balancing), so each of the 15 trained networks is run on 100 samples of the initial stocks, giving $15 \times 100 = 1500$ samples of the flow tensor, of which the published dataset reports the mean and one standard deviation. `train_ensemble` does the same at toy scale: 5 networks times 10 draws gives 50 samples, with each initial cell perturbed by a log-normal factor whose scale is the cell's balancing uncertainty. The next cell separates the two sources in the toy, to see how much each contributes to the total spread.

```{python}
#| label: cell-mf-variance
S_ = fit_mem["samples"].sum(axis=2)                       # (50, years, n, n)
members, draws = fit_mem["cfg"].members, fit_mem["cfg"].pushforward_draws
grouped = S_.reshape(members, draws, *S_.shape[1:])
logF = np.log(np.maximum(grouped[:, :, :, off], 1e-9))   # log flows, per edge
within = logF.var(axis=1, ddof=1).mean()                  # initial-stock pushforward
between = logF.mean(axis=1).var(axis=0, ddof=1).mean()    # ensemble disagreement
print(f"variance of log flows from the initial-stock pushforward (within members): {within:.4f}")
print(f"variance of log flows from ensemble disagreement (between members):       {between:.4f}")
```

In the toy the initial-stock pushforward contributes almost nothing (`{python} f"{within:.4f}"` against `{python} f"{between:.4f}"` for the disagreement between members), because the initial-stock uncertainties produced by the balancing are small and the flows depend on the stock only through a log-scale input feature. Almost all of the spread comes from the ensemble, that is, from the ill-posedness of the problem. That ordering need not carry over to the real data, where the initial stocks are far noisier, and it is a reason to keep both sources in the pipeline.

## Alternative Viewpoints and Limits {#sec-mf-alternatives}

* **Identifiability is the central worry.** Estimating $T_{ijk}$ from aggregates is ill-posed, and the network's smoothness and the covariates are doing real work. If the covariates say Syrians are unlikely to move from Turkey to Germany, the output will reflect that prior. The ensemble spread does not account for such prior-driven error, as the toy shows (@sec-mf-results). Independent validation data, not model fit, is the evidence.
* **The covariates are themselves estimates.** GDP, trade, and refugee stock series have gaps that the paper fills by extrapolation. Errors in them propagate unmodelled, apart from the initial stocks.
* **The loss design embeds judgement.** Net migration targets get weight one; the source priority order (Quantmig, then national statistics, then Facebook) decides which dataset wins in an overlap; and the clamp range is a hyperparameter. A different but defensible choice changes the answer for badly measured corridors.
* **Definitions of "a move" differ.** The paper restricts to sources with a one-year definition, but even those are not identical. A neural network that matches several definitions at once learns a compromise, not a definition.
* **Simpler baselines deserve a fair trial.** The paper compares with stock differencing and demographic accounting. A reader should also try a regularised gravity model plus the accounting identity, with a latent state added only if it wins, to know what the neural network is buying.
* **The same pattern applies elsewhere.** Hidden-state estimation from several aggregates shows up wherever a flow is unobservable but conserved: epidemiology (infections from reported cases, hospitalisations, and deaths), supply chains (shipments from inventories and sales), and finance (flows from balance sheets). The ingredients are the same: write the identity, parameterise the unknown with a covariate-driven network, give it a memory, weight sources by trust, and report an ensemble.

## Summary {#sec-mf-summary}

The paper's contribution is a way of posing the estimation problem as much as a model. The flow tensor $T_{ijk}$ is never observed, but its aggregates (stocks, net migration, corridor totals) are, by instruments that disagree. Writing the exact accounting identity of @eq-mf-stock inside a recurrent network lets one training run reconcile all of them, with the network's covariate-driven smoothness selecting one solution among the many consistent ones. Four supporting choices make it work: the symmetrised Yeo-Johnson transform so heavy-tailed and signed values can share a loss; matching *differences* of stocks so census level errors do not leak in; weights built from the uncertainty of each source; and an ensemble with an initial-stock pushforward to put an interval on the answer.

The synthetic replication shows the idea working and shows its limits. The accounting identity holds to rounding error, the recurrent ensemble fits its targets much better than a memoryless one, it orders corridors it never saw correctly, and it reproduces the slow decay after a shock where a memoryless model cannot. It also shows what the method does not give for free: unobserved small corridors are underestimated by a sizeable margin, the ensemble band is too narrow because it cannot see bias shared by all members, and elasticities are not recovered when the covariates vary too little to identify them. Looking inside the recurrence adds a further lesson. The model has two recurrent paths: an ungated Elman latent block, whose gradient vanishes within a few years for most edges, and the accounting identity, an additive carry with a forget gate fixed near 0.99 by mortality. Replacing the latent block by a gated, GRU-style update keeps the latent gradient alive across all 20 years and, in this run, improves the fit and the post-shock recovery. These are the reasons the paper validates on held-out real flows, and the reasons a reader adopting the pattern for a new problem should build a synthetic world first and measure what comes back.
